# Cortical stimulation during SEEG

A reading edition for epilepsy physicians, colleagues, and fellows: anatomy, phenomenology, mechanisms, study findings, and the reasoning behind interpretation.

Colleague-review draft · Version 2026-09-17.1. Named clinical review remains pending. Operating settings and clinical procedures require the effective local protocol. Sources distinguish primary findings, conference teaching, hypotheses, and original teaching synthesis.

<a id="chapter-1"></a>

# Chapter 1 — What Direct Cortical Stimulation Can Teach Us

## A perturbation experiment inside a clinical investigation

Direct cortical stimulation (DCS) changes activity in tissue reached by an electrical field and in the circuits connected to it. During stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG), implanted depth contacts make such experiments possible in sulcal, mesial, and deep cortical regions that are difficult to reach with surface electrodes. The resulting movement, sensation, interruption of performance, electrical response, or induced seizure can answer different questions. Understanding the question is the first step toward understanding the finding.

Kahane's historical account places two purposes side by side: reproducing a patient's seizure experience and identifying functions relevant to surgery. That distinction persists in the final ACNS technical standard, which addresses both functional mapping and seizure induction. [Philippe Kahane — Historical aspects of DCS in epilepsy](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25340/historical-aspects-of-dcs-in-epilepsy-philippe-kahane); [ACNS, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39946166/). 

## Three questions that deserve separate answers

### 1. What function can be altered here?

Functional mapping concerns a change in perception, movement, language, memory, or another assessed function. The meaningful comparison is with the patient's performance or experience without stimulation under the relevant conditions. A naming disturbance does not need to resemble the patient's seizures to be a useful functional finding.

A positive finding may reveal participation in a function, but the clinical meaning depends on the task and the tissue reached. A picture-naming error could arise at several stages: seeing the picture, recognizing the object, accessing its name, planning speech, or producing it. The statement “language was affected” is less informative than the actual task and error. The international practice survey treats functional/cognitive assessment separately from epileptogenicity assessment. [Cockle et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10472359/). 

### 2. Does an induced event illuminate the seizure hypothesis?

An induced sensory experience, a fragment of a habitual seizure, and a fuller electroclinical seizure offer different kinds of comparison with spontaneous events. Here the relevant questions concern the patient's familiar sequence, the recorded EEG evolution, and the relationship among stimulation site, induced onset, and spontaneous onset.

The comparison can be informative without being complete. Reproducing one familiar symptom does not reproduce every part of a seizure. Conversely, a difference in expression does not automatically make the event meaningless: the difference itself needs description. [Chapter 3](#chapter-3) develops these distinctions, and [Chapter 17](#chapter-17) reviews the outcome literature. 

### 3. What electrical or network response follows stimulation?

An evoked potential after a pulse, a change in spectral activity, and an afterdischarge are different measurements. Some experiments are designed to study connectivity or excitability rather than to produce a conscious experience. An electrical response with no reported sensation can still be a meaningful experimental result. A sensation with limited recorded change presents a different interpretive problem. The measurement and the clinical observation should remain distinguishable. [Olivier David — What do we do when stimulating the brain: activation or inhibition?](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25342/what-do-we-do-when-stimulating-the-brain-activation-or-inhibition-olivier-david). 

## Activation, inhibition, and disruption are different levels of description

It is tempting to imagine stimulation as turning a small patch of cortex “on” or “off.” David's lecture explains why that picture is incomplete. Electrical stimulation can recruit axons, drive connected regions, and generate temporally evolving responses containing excitatory and inhibitory contributions. The response to a train depends on how successive pulses interact with those dynamics. A clinical interruption therefore does not establish that every local neuron was inhibited; a positive movement does not establish that only local cell bodies were activated. [Olivier David — What do we do when stimulating the brain: activation or inhibition?](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25342/what-do-we-do-when-stimulating-the-brain-activation-or-inhibition-olivier-david). 

The most useful distinction is between **what the nervous system was perturbed with**, **what was recorded**, and **what the patient did or experienced**. These are linked observations at different scales. Interpreting them as the same thing creates false certainty about mechanism.

For example, failure to speak during stimulation is a behavioral result. “Speech production was disrupted under this task” is an interpretation close to that result. “The language center was switched off” adds an unproven cellular mechanism and an oversimplified anatomical model. This example is an editorial illustration, not a diagnostic classification.

## The anatomical terms behind a presurgical discussion

| Term | What it refers to in this manual | Why it is easy to confuse |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulated contacts | The contacts used to deliver current. | Their labels may be used as shorthand for tissue that the electrical field reaches more broadly. |
| Recorded onset | The earliest seizure change identified in the available recording. | SEEG records sampled tissue, not every possible origin. |
| Symptom-producing region/network | Tissue whose activity or disruption contributes to the experience or behavior. | A symptom can emerge after seizure activity has propagated. |
| Functionally important tissue | Tissue whose contribution is examined by a particular functional assessment. | A result on one task does not test every possible function. |
| Epileptogenic-zone hypothesis | The clinical hypothesis about tissue/network responsible for seizures and relevant to treatment. | It is inferred from converging evidence, rather than directly measured by one stimulation response. |

This is explanatory terminology grounded in the conference's historical and network discussions. It does not assign a zone to a patient or define a surgical boundary. [Philippe Kahane — Historical aspects of DCS in epilepsy](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25340/historical-aspects-of-dcs-in-epilepsy-philippe-kahane); [Sandor Beniczky & Olalla Urdanibia-Centelles — How to move forward with recommendations and guidelines on seizure semiology in clinical practice](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25382/how-to-move-forward-with-recommendations-and-guidelines-on-seizure-semiology-in-clinical-practice-sandor-beniczky-olalla-urdanibia-centelles). 

## Why SEEG observations can be powerful and incomplete

SEEG contacts are implanted to test a clinical hypothesis. Coverage is therefore selective. A richly sampled region can produce many recorded responses, while another region may have only a few tested contacts or none. Response counts in a stimulation series must be read with that sampling pattern in mind. A large number of reports from one region may reflect both biology and opportunity to observe it.

The same contact can also participate in several experiments. A site associated with an auditory sensation during one trial may be examined with a language task in another. Anatomical labels are not mutually exclusive bins for symptoms. This is why the manual contains both symptom-oriented chapters and region-oriented chapters: they answer complementary reading questions. 

## A worked comparison

*Hypothetical teaching example:* stimulation near temporal cortex produces a familiar smell without a fuller habitual sequence. In a separate trial, a naming task is interrupted. The smell belongs to a comparison with spontaneous semiology; the naming error belongs to a functional comparison with baseline. Both findings deserve explanation. Neither can stand in for the other, and neither alone specifies the treatment boundary.

## What to carry forward

DCS adds experimentally elicited evidence to a larger clinical investigation. Its value increases when the purpose, response, anatomy, and measurement context are clear. Anatomical associations should be taught in detail, while keeping the path from an observed effect to a patient-level conclusion visible.

## Sources and reading notes

This chapter combines the cited ACNS abstract and practice survey with attributed PAROS teaching on history, mechanism, and semiology. The network and terminology explanations are editorial synthesis of those sources. Transcript passages were checked selectively; no VOD verification is claimed. The reading edition supports learning and clinical discussion; effective local protocols govern stimulation procedures.

# Chapter 2 — Stimulation Parameters, Afterdischarges, and Safety Concepts

## Why settings belong in the explanation

A response is not produced by a current value alone. The pulse, its duration, the repetition pattern, the contacts, and the tissue relationship form the experiment. Two reports that both say “5 mA” may describe different electrical exposures. This chapter explains the quantities and study methods so that readers can interpret the literature and discuss their local practice intelligently. It does not replace the approved device-specific protocol.

Schuele's PAROS lecture repeatedly emphasizes contact geometry and the difficulty of comparing studies that omit functional or afterdischarge thresholds. The central lesson is that a familiar setting cannot be assumed equivalent across electrode designs. [Stephan Schuele — Parameters and safety issues of DCS](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25341/parameters-and-safety-issues-of-dcs-stephan-schuele). 

## Read the parameter set as a whole

| Quantity | Meaning | What it changes in the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Current amplitude | The current during a pulse phase, commonly reported in mA. | It is only one component of charge delivery. |
| Phase duration | How long one phase lasts. | A longer phase at the same current delivers more charge. |
| Pulse frequency | How often pulses are delivered. | It changes temporal interaction among successive responses. |
| Train duration | How long the pulse sequence continues. | It changes exposure and the time available to observe a task or induced event. |
| Waveform | The shape and polarity of the phases. | Charge balance and phase conventions affect comparisons. |
| Montage | The contacts involved in delivery and return. | The field depends on the electrical arrangement and surrounding tissue. |
| Geometry | Contact surface, shape, spacing, and tissue position. | The same displayed settings can correspond to different geometric charge densities. |

The terms are standard explanatory quantities in the source record. Symmetric, charge-balanced biphasic pulses are commonly described, but waveform terminology must be read carefully: “pulse width” may be used inconsistently for a phase or the complete pulse. A method section should make that convention explicit. [Drane et al., 2021, stimulation parameters](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2021.627981/full). 

## Charge per phase and geometric charge density

For a rectangular constant-current phase:

**Charge per phase = current × phase duration.**

With current in mA and duration in ms, the product is in µC. Geometric charge density per phase is this charge divided by the active contact's geometric surface area in cm². The adjective “geometric” matters: it is a calculation using the stated contact area, not a complete measurement of current distribution within brain tissue. [Cockle et al., 2023, Methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10472359/). 

*Mathematical teaching example:* if phase duration doubles while current and area stay fixed, charge per phase and geometric charge density both double. If contact area halves while the pulse stays fixed, geometric charge density doubles. These proportional examples explain the dependency without suggesting a usable clinical setting.

The final ACNS abstract states that a maximum safe charge-density limit is **not established for stereotactic depth electrodes**. A surface-electrode limit, a historical table, or a limit in another modality cannot silently become a universal SEEG ceiling. [ACNS, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39946166/). 

Charge-density calculations depend on the stated electrode dimensions. Current hardware and approved local limits must be established independently; historical tables are not operating instructions.

## Low-frequency trains, high-frequency trains, and single pulses

The literature commonly distinguishes approximately **1-Hz low-frequency** and **50-Hz high-frequency** stimulation paradigms. These labels identify families of methods, not equivalent electrical doses. Different investigators also use different phase durations, currents, train lengths, and purposes. A low-frequency train used to induce a seizure and an experiment averaging responses to isolated pulses may share a nominal frequency yet have different endpoints and analyses. 

High-frequency paradigms are frequently described in functional mapping; low-frequency paradigms are also used for seizure induction and in particular cortical contexts. Source authors disagree about optimal applications. The useful teaching is to compare the complete method and its goal, rather than memorizing a frequency as universally appropriate. [Arya et al., 2020, stimulation settings](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2020.611291/full). [Chapter 17](#chapter-17) explains single-pulse research in more detail. 

## Afterdischarges: electrical events that change the experiment

An afterdischarge is rhythmic epileptiform activity following stimulation that differs from the prestimulation recording. It can remain limited or spread, and can complicate attribution of a clinical response. It is not automatically synonymous with an induced clinical seizure. The detailed explanatory definition in the existing source packet came from the [ACNS May 2024 draft, §4.2](https://www.acns.org/UserFiles/file/ACNSESMTechStandards_DRAFT5.14.24_v1.pdf); it has not been checked against the final full text. 

Hamer's lecture explains why afterdischarge studies are difficult to combine. Definitions, morphology categories, current thresholds, electrodes, regions, pathology, and seizure emergence are not reported consistently. He notes possible regional and lesion-related influences, including reports involving hippocampus and malformations of cortical development, but does not establish a universally predictive morphology or threshold. [Hajo Hamer — The issue of DCS-elicited discharges and how to deal with](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25343/the-issue-of-dcs-elicited-discharges-and-how-to-deal-with-hajo-hamer). 

Gollwitzer's study explicitly analyzed duration, propagation, morphology, and seizure emergence. Its research definition used a duration cutoff; that cutoff is part of its methodology, not a universally adopted boundary between harmless and dangerous responses. [Gollwitzer et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30269940/).

### Why electrical context changes a functional conclusion

Suppose a patient makes a naming error while a discharge spreads beyond the stimulated pair. The event still contains useful observations: the stimulation, the error, and the discharge. What becomes harder is attributing the error exclusively to the immediately stimulated tissue. This is an interpretive limitation, not a reason to erase the trial or convert it into an uncomplicated positive site.

A study's exclusion of trials with afterdischarges may help isolate an experimental effect, but it also changes which observations enter the analysis. That selection should be retained when comparing studies.

## Functional threshold and afterdischarge threshold

The current at which a functional response appears and the current at which an afterdischarge appears are different measurements under specified conditions. Schuele uses their relationship to explain a practical problem: if unwanted electrical activity emerges before a clear functional response, the available experiment is constrained. A missing functional response then cannot be interpreted as though the whole parameter space had been tested. [Stephan Schuele — Parameters and safety issues of DCS](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25341/parameters-and-safety-issues-of-dcs-stephan-schuele). 

Thresholds vary with patient, task, method, and electrode conditions. A research average does not determine an individual threshold, and reaching a study's reported maximum is not a clearance criterion.

## Children: development changes the experiment

Eltze's pediatric review emphasizes age-related differences in elicitation thresholds, language assessment, and cooperation. Younger children may differ from older children in both biological excitability and the ability to understand or perform a task. Those two explanations should remain distinct. A lower yield of task-defined language effects may reflect the assessment as well as the network being sampled. [Christin Eltze — DCS in children : are elicited responses different from adults, and how to do?](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25345/dcs-in-children-are-elicited-responses-different-from-adults-and-how-to-do-christin-eltze). 

Pediatric findings therefore deserve their own patient and method context. Adult settings or adult task assumptions should not be transferred merely by relabeling the patient group. This chapter teaches the reason for adaptation; the clinical team supplies the approved pediatric procedure.

## What to carry forward

Interpret settings as a complete physical and temporal description. Keep functional response, afterdischarge, and induced seizure separate. Read safety limits in the electrode and evidence context in which they were established. The local protocol determines escalation, interruption, monitoring, and rescue treatment; understanding the concepts helps clinicians evaluate that protocol without substituting an unverified table for it.

## Sources and reading notes

PAROS25341, 25343 and 25345 provide attributed teaching; the parameter explanation is supported by the cited methods literature and survey. The ACNS final abstract now directly supports the depth-electrode charge-limit statement above. Detailed afterdischarge-definition passages remain identified as draft-derived. No local hardware assumption or clinical approval was inferred.

# Chapter 3 — Interpreting Responses: From Observation to Clinical Inference

## Interpretation is a chain of claims

A stimulation trial can support several statements of increasing ambition: a change occurred; it was temporally related to stimulation; it involved the tested function; the sampled tissue or its connections contributed; and the finding changes a clinical hypothesis. Each additional statement requires additional reasoning. A dramatic response does not remove the need to examine that chain.

The goal is not to avoid interpretation. It is to make the interpretation understandable. The reader should be able to see where the observation ends, where the anatomical explanation begins, and what other evidence is needed for a patient-level conclusion. 

## Functional findings: task, baseline, and specificity

In functional assessment, the reference is the task and baseline performance. A response does not become less relevant merely because it differs from spontaneous seizures. Conversely, an error that resembles a seizure-associated difficulty is not automatically a functional map. These are different comparisons. [Cockle et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10472359/). 

### Why task analysis matters

Consider picture naming. The observed response combines visual input, object recognition, semantic knowledge, lexical retrieval, speech planning, and articulation. A failure somewhere in that chain may look like “could not name.” It matters whether the patient could still describe the object's use, recognize it, read a word, repeat speech, or move the articulators—but those additional observations depend on which assessments were actually performed. One cannot retrospectively infer them from silence.

The anatomical interpretation should therefore name the supported component. [Chapter 11](#chapter-11) explains language and praxis distinctions. This decomposition is teaching synthesis, not a proposed task battery or a rule for declaring a site indispensable. 

### Repetition adds evidence, not a universal label

If a similar error occurs in several trials, its consistency is informative. If it appears only once, uncertainty differs. But the meaning of repetition depends on task difficulty, baseline errors, stimulation conditions, and electrical context. The survey literature documents differences in positive-site criteria across centers. “Reproducible” should therefore describe what was observed before it is treated as a shared clinical criterion. 

## Induced events: resemblance has several dimensions

An induced event can resemble a spontaneous seizure in subjective onset, observable behavior, sequence, duration, or EEG evolution. These dimensions can agree or diverge. A familiar epigastric sensation followed by a different motor expression is not equivalent to a full reproduction of the habitual sequence. An apparently similar behavior with different sampled electrographic onset also deserves a more detailed account than “concordant.”

Mahizhnan and colleagues examined within-patient semiology using habitual/atypical classifications and aura, fragment, and fuller clinical expressions. Their framework illustrates the value of preserving the extent of similarity. Its study-specific definitions are not a universal score. [Mahizhnan et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41603150/). 

### Three anatomical observations that should remain distinct

1. **Where current was delivered.**
2. **Where the induced seizure first appeared in the recording.**
3. **Where spontaneous seizures first appeared in the recording.**

Reindl's study analyzed stimulation contacts and induced onset contacts separately. Their relationship to spontaneous onset and later resection was part of the investigation. Treating these as one location loses the experiment's structure. [Reindl et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-92241-z). 

## Local recruitment, spread, and network access

The visible response can involve tissue beyond the current-delivery pair. David's mechanism lecture describes local and remote responses to stimulation; Beniczky and Urdanibia-Centelles emphasize that semiology can reflect propagation into a symptom-producing network. Together these ideas explain why an anatomically plausible response may identify network participation without locating the beginning of a spontaneous seizure. [Olivier David — What do we do when stimulating the brain: activation or inhibition?](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25342/what-do-we-do-when-stimulating-the-brain-activation-or-inhibition-olivier-david); [Sandor Beniczky & Olalla Urdanibia-Centelles — How to move forward with recommendations and guidelines on seizure semiology in clinical practice](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25382/how-to-move-forward-with-recommendations-and-guidelines-on-seizure-semiology-in-clinical-practice-sandor-beniczky-olalla-urdanibia-centelles). 

This does not make anatomy irrelevant. It changes the question from “Which single structure must this symptom mean?” to “Which structures have produced this response, under what methods, and how does that fit the patient's sampled network?” The symptom and regional chapters supply the details needed for that discussion.

## What outcome associations add

Cuello Oderiz's 103-patient, two-center observational study associated typical stimulation-induced electroclinical seizures and the extent of resection of stimulation-informed onset tissue with outcome. Its analyzed events required a clinical and evolving electrographic component. Auras without that EEG component were outside the primary outcome definition. [Cuello Oderiz et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6563597/). 

The association is meaningful evidence, but several links remain: why a patient was selected for invasive study, what was implanted, which stimulations were performed, how treatment was chosen, and what tissue was ultimately treated. These factors make an observational outcome association different from a prospective test of a rule applied independently to a new patient.

A three-study meta-analysis found an imprecise pooled association with favorable outcome that did not reach statistical significance. That result neither proves absence of value nor provides a calibrated prediction rule. The small evidence base and differences in stimulation paradigms limit the inference. [Fox et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40466207/). [Chapter 17](#chapter-17) treats these studies in detail. 

## Absent responses: what was actually tested?

An absent observed response can arise in different experimental situations. The relevant function may not have been assessed; the task may have been insensitive to the affected component; the tissue may have been incompletely sampled; or interpretation may have been constrained by stimulation and electrical conditions. These are reasons to describe the experiment, not a checklist that establishes why any particular result was absent.

“No reportable sensation” also differs from “no measurable physiological effect.” Duong's insular study provides a useful example: some sampled tissue showed task-related high-frequency activity without reportable subjective effects during stimulation. This cautions against treating subjective silence as a complete description of cortical function. [Duong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10893903/). 

## A worked interpretation

*Hypothetical teaching example:* a patient reports the familiar initial sensation after stimulation of one temporal pair. A discharge then appears in several recorded regions, but the usual later behavioral sequence is absent. The defensible description preserves the familiar sensation, the sampled electrical evolution, and the incomplete clinical expression. An interpretation may discuss the relationship to the seizure hypothesis. It should not silently convert the observation into “the complete habitual seizure arose from this pair.”

A separate trial interrupting naming is interpreted against baseline naming and its electrical context. The lack of seizure resemblance in that trial is irrelevant to whether language performance changed.

## What to carry forward

Useful interpretation is explicit about the comparison being made. Anatomical association, functional contribution, resemblance to spontaneous seizures, and association with postoperative outcome are different claims. Each can teach us something, but none should be smuggled into another through an unexplained “positive,” “negative,” or “reliable” label.

# Chapter 4 — Before Stimulation: Designing an Interpretable Session

## Planning begins with a question that can be answered

A stimulation session can contain several purposes: testing functional contribution, reproducing a symptom or seizure, and measuring a physiological response. The aim of planning is to connect the intended question with an observation that could answer it. A list of contacts and settings does not by itself make that connection.

The separate purposes appear in the ACNS standard and international practice survey. This chapter explains their consequences for study design and clinical reasoning. Local procedures determine how the team prepares and runs an actual session. [ACNS, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39946166/); [Cockle et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10472359/). 

## The implantation hypothesis and the stimulation question

SEEG coverage reflects a presurgical hypothesis. That hypothesis explains why regions were sampled; it does not establish the function or seizure role of every implanted contact. The stimulation question can be narrower than the implantation question. A trajectory placed to distinguish seizure hypotheses may also sample tissue relevant to language or perception.

For teaching, distinguish three descriptions: the hypothesized network, the tissue actually sampled, and the response one intends to assess. Keeping them separate prevents the implantation plan from becoming a circular explanation of a later finding. A response can support, complicate, or leave the hypothesis unresolved. 

### Sampling has a shape

A depth-electrode trajectory samples a line through selected tissue; a surface array samples a different geometry. Neither produces uniform coverage of all potentially relevant cortex. Sulci, neighboring gyri, white matter, and the position of both contacts in a stimulated pair matter. A contact called “insula” on a summary diagram may need more precise anatomical description before its response can be compared with an insular study.

The auditory attribution dispute in [Chapter 7](#chapter-7) illustrates the consequence: an apparent insular response may involve adjacent superior temporal cortex when reviewed in individual anatomy. This is why regional expectations should be tied to actual sampling rather than to a label alone. [Duong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10893903/). 

## Baseline is part of the experiment

A functional task needs a meaningful baseline. If an item cannot be named before stimulation, failure to name it during stimulation cannot be interpreted like a newly induced failure. If the patient can perform an easy task but not a demanding one, the choice changes which disturbance could be detected.

The principle extends beyond accuracy. Response time, comprehension of the instructions, sensory access to the material, and fatigue can shape the observed performance. These are explanatory sources of variation, not newly mandated measurements. The cognitive-mapping literature emphasizes individualized assessment and the relation between task demands and sampled circuits. [Drane et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2021.627981/full). 

### Match the assessment to the function

A task is a probe, not the function itself. Picture naming samples several visual and linguistic processes. Reading samples a different combination. Counting may reveal interruption of overlearned speech while missing difficulties with naming or comprehension. The interpretation becomes stronger when it states which demand was present and which component changed.

Similarly, asking whether an induced event resembles a habitual seizure addresses another comparison entirely. It does not substitute for a functional baseline. Chapters 1 and 3 establish that distinction; Chapter 11 expands it for language.

## Patient-specific factors are part of the design

Eltze's pediatric lecture connects developmental age, elicitation thresholds, task performance, preparation, and cooperation. A young child may be unable to sustain the same experimental conditions as an adult. The failure to obtain a task-defined response can therefore have more than one explanation. The lesson is to make those conditions visible, rather than to presume that an adult task has measured the same construct in every child. [Christin Eltze — DCS in children : are elicited responses different from adults, and how to do?](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25345/dcs-in-children-are-elicited-responses-different-from-adults-and-how-to-do-christin-eltze). 

Language background and the cognitive profile similarly affect what an assessment means. This chapter does not select a language battery or pediatric adaptation; it explains why the responsible team needs one appropriate to the patient and the question.

## Anticipating what must remain reviewable

A finding is easier to interpret later when its relation to stimulation, task performance, and EEG is recoverable. If the observation is subjective, the original description carries information that a later shorthand may lose. If the finding is an action, video may reveal its sequence. If the concern is a discharge, its electrical evolution is central.

These are reasons to consider the capture method during planning. They do not define a universal minimum dataset or assign institutional recording roles. The source record supports the rationale more strongly than a single required implementation. 

## A planning comparison

*Hypothetical teaching example:* the broad question is whether a sampled temporal network contributes to a patient's seizures and language. One planned observation concerns the first familiar seizure sensation and the induced EEG sequence. Another concerns performance on a language assessment the patient can perform at baseline. The two observations may involve neighboring contacts, but they need different comparisons and different interpretations.

If the language assessment is never performed, an absence of spontaneous speech difficulty during other trials cannot be treated as its substitute. If no familiar event is induced, a functional language response still retains its own significance.

## What to carry forward

An interpretable session connects a clinical question, a sampled anatomical context, an assessment, and a recoverable observation. Preparation is not just logistical: it determines what the eventual response can mean. The local protocol supplies the actual safety preparation, contact selection, task administration, and role allocation. 

## Sources and reading notes

The cited survey, cognitive-mapping review, primary anatomical example, and pediatric conference teaching support the rationale. The planning comparisons are editorial illustrations. This chapter is explanatory teaching rather than a clearance checklist.

# Chapter 5 — During Stimulation: Understanding the Patient’s Experience

## Four observations can coexist in one trial

A patient reports a sensation; the team observes behavior; the recording shows an electrical event; and clinicians form an interpretation. Each may be accurate, but they describe different aspects of the trial. A useful account keeps all four recognizable.

This chapter expands that distinction into a way of thinking about observations. It is not a new interview script or monitoring protocol. The purpose is to help readers understand what is lost when a complicated response becomes a single label. 

## Subjective experience and visible behavior

Some responses are accessible mainly through the patient's report: a smell, familiarity, an internal sense of movement, or fear. Others are externally visible: a limb movement, laughter, a speech interruption, or a change in task performance. A visible action does not automatically reveal the corresponding feeling. Laughter can occur with or without reported amusement; a movement may occur with or without an accompanying urge.

The distinction is developed in the PAROS talks on affect-related behavior, motor automatisms, and bodily illusions. [Georgia Ramantani — Affect-related behaviours (laughing, crying, pouting, biting, fighting, screaming, humming, kissing)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25356/affect-related-behaviours-laughing-crying-pouting-biting-fighting-screaming-humming-kissing-georgia-ramantani); [Sylvain Rheims — Motor automatisms (axial, distal, proximal, wandering)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25357/motor-automatisms-axial-distal-proximal-wandering-sylvain-rheims); [Lars Etholm — Bodily illusions (autoscopy, heautoscopy, OBE, levity)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25350/bodily-illusions-autoscopy-heautoscopy-obe-levity-lars-etholm). 

### Description preserves information that a label discards

Compare three hypothetical descriptions: “sensory response,” “a tingling sensation,” and “tingling confined to two fingers.” The latter descriptions add quality and distribution. None is automatically a better localization rule, but each permits a more precise comparison with the stimulation literature.

Likewise, “speech stopped” does not tell the reader whether speech was being requested, whether the patient understood, whether an attempted response was articulated, or whether another behavior interrupted the task. The language chapter explains why these distinctions matter for anatomy. 

## Time and sequence are part of the phenomenon

A response beginning during the train, one first reported after it, and one evolving with an afterdischarge raise different attribution questions. The exact temporal relationship may be uncertain, particularly for subjective experiences recalled afterwards. That uncertainty should remain visible.

Sequence also matters. A familiar sensation followed by an unfamiliar action is a compound observation. If only the final action is retained, the early semiology disappears. If the whole event is labeled “habitual” based only on the first sensation, the difference disappears. This is why within-patient induced-event studies distinguish partial from fuller expressions. [Mahizhnan et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41603150/). 

## Electrical context changes attribution

Stimulation artifact, an evoked response, afterdischarge, and an evolving seizure are not interchangeable EEG phenomena. Their relationship to the clinical observation determines how narrowly an effect can be attributed. A task error accompanied by spreading epileptiform activity has a different interpretation from an isolated task-linked change under otherwise comparable conditions.

Hamer's afterdischarge review emphasizes variation in definitions, morphology, and reported methods. An impressive-looking discharge should not be named a clinical seizure merely because it is striking. Conversely, electrical and clinical evolution need to be described when they do support an induced electroclinical event. [Hajo Hamer — The issue of DCS-elicited discharges and how to deal with](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25343/the-issue-of-dcs-elicited-discharges-and-how-to-deal-with-hajo-hamer). 

The fact that another study excluded afterdischarge-associated trials explains its analysis; it does not by itself decide how a clinical unit should classify or manage its own trial.

## Words can overstate the evidence

### “Positive”

Positive for what: an induced sensation, a functional task effect, a discharge, or resemblance to a habitual seizure? The term is useful only when the question and criterion are known. It should not act as a universal shortcut across all forms of stimulation.

### “Negative”

No reported sensation, no observed behavior change, no task error, and no induced seizure are different absences. A trial without an administered memory task has not demonstrated intact memory under stimulation. A silent response does not establish that a region has no function.

### “Reproducible”

Repetition describes consistency under the conditions tested. Its meaning depends on what repeated: the patient's exact experience, a broad category, a task error, or an EEG pattern. The number of trials and response criteria in the literature vary. Repetition is evidence to explain, not a substitute for explaining the finding. [Cockle et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10472359/). 

## Neutral description and the risk of suggestion

A detailed vocabulary is valuable for the clinician, but it can bias interpretation if a response is forced into an expected category. Knowing that posterior insular stimulation has produced pain does not establish that a patient's ambiguous “strange feeling” was pain. Similarly, expecting a mesial temporal memory response can encourage overreading a nonspecific sense of familiarity.

The teaching principle is to preserve the account that was actually obtained, including ambiguity. This is an editorial caution about interpretation, not a claim that a particular prompt wording has been validated. The manual's existing neutral-prompt row remains unresolved as an operational script. 

## A worked account of a mixed response

*Hypothetical teaching example:* during stimulation, the patient smiles and then laughs. Afterwards the patient describes surprise without amusement. A later summary that says “mirth elicited” adds a feeling the patient did not report. “Laughter without reported mirth” preserves the difference, provided that the report was actually obtained; absent documentation of mirth is not the same as a denial of mirth.

The observation could then be compared with the affect-related stimulation literature in Chapter 9. Anatomical interpretation would still require the contacts, task context, timing, and electrical record.

## What to carry forward

The patient's experience, observed action, EEG, and later explanation can support each other without becoming interchangeable. Precision begins with preserving those distinctions. The richer descriptions in Chapters 7–11 supply language for learning from what occurred, while local protocols determine actual interviewing, testing, monitoring, and response management. 

## Sources and reading notes

This chapter uses the established observation framework, cited study definitions, and attributed conference teaching. Hypothetical examples demonstrate loss of information through labeling; they are not patient cases or clinical forms. No mandatory trial count, task battery, prompt sequence, or monitoring requirement is introduced.

# Chapter 6 — After Stimulation: Turning Observations into an Account

## The record should survive a change of mind

A clinical impression can change as new seizures are recorded, anatomy is reconsidered, or other evidence becomes available. The original stimulation observations should remain intelligible through that change. A report that records only the conclusion cannot easily be reinterpreted.

The purpose of this chapter is to explain the information behind a useful account. Chapter 16 considers how several findings are read together. Neither chapter substitutes for an institution's documentation requirements. 

## Preserve the event before summarizing its meaning

A stimulation account has an experimental context and an observed result. The context includes what was assessed and the relevant stimulation, task, anatomical, and electrical conditions. The result may contain the patient's experience, observed behavior, task performance, and EEG evolution. The interpretation explains their significance.

These components need not appear under prescribed headings. What matters is whether a later reader can distinguish them. An anatomical conclusion without the response description is hard to check; a symptom description without its experimental context is hard to compare. 

### Why “positive temporal stimulation” is insufficient

The phrase leaves several possibilities open: an auditory percept, a naming error, a familiar aura, an electroclinical seizure, or an isolated discharge. Even if everyone present knew what it meant, a colleague reading later may not. Naming the observed phenomenon and its tested question preserves the evidence that supports the impression.

This is an editorial principle. It does not establish a new local vocabulary or positive-site criterion.

## Functional findings and induced seizures retain different comparisons

A functional account relates the observed change to the actual task and baseline. If the task was picture naming, the reader should not be led to believe that every language function was examined. If performance was limited before stimulation, that limitation belongs to the interpretation.

An induced-event account compares the clinical and electrographic expression with spontaneous events. Mahizhnan's within-patient study illustrates why a familiar aura, a fragment, and a fuller habitual expression should remain distinguishable. [Mahizhnan et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41603150/). 

A functional effect can matter without habituality. A habitual sensation can matter without constituting a complete functional assessment. Combining both under “concordant positive response” can conceal rather than summarize the findings.

## Anatomy should retain the experiment's resolution

An electrode name is not always a sufficient anatomical description. The individual position of the current-delivery pair, nearby structures, and the location of recorded responses can all matter. If a later anatomical review changes a contact from an apparent insular label to adjacent superior temporal cortex, the original auditory observation remains; its anatomical attribution changes. [Duong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10893903/). 

This is a practical example of why a good account separates data from interpretation. It also explains why manuscript summaries should not discard uncertainty in contact localization.

## A richer account is not necessarily a longer impression

*Hypothetical teaching example:* an account describes an induced familiar smell, a recorded discharge with a stated distribution, and an incomplete resemblance to the patient's usual sequence. The impression can then say that the event contributes evidence relevant to the seizure hypothesis while identifying what did not match. That impression may be short because the underlying observations remain available.

By contrast, expanding “positive stimulation” into several paragraphs of confidence language does not improve the account if the actual event remains unspecified. Detail should preserve the experiment, not merely amplify the conclusion. 

## Explain conflicting and incomplete findings

Different trials may produce different expressions. The question is not simply which result to keep. Differences may concern task, current-delivery pair, electrical context, symptom component, or the patient's state. A report can describe the pattern without assigning a universal confidence score.

An absent response should likewise retain its scope. “No familiar event was elicited under the tested conditions” does not mean “the region cannot participate in the seizure network.” “No naming error on the administered assessment” is narrower than “language-negative cortex.” Those examples illustrate the importance of matching the conclusion to the observation; they do not validate a negative-response rule. 

## Why consistent data help research

The same precision that helps a colleague read a report also helps researchers compare observations. Cockle's survey documents substantial variation in stimulation practice and classification. If studies use different meanings of a positive response or omit the conditions that produced it, pooled anatomical conclusions become harder to interpret. [Cockle et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10472359/). 

The PAROS platform and probabilistic-atlas talks propose ways to organize and reuse intracranial data. Their broader lesson is that a response acquires meaning through its context: anatomy, paradigm, task, and source population. A count of reported responses without a denominator of tested opportunities is not a patient-specific probability. [Philippe Ryvlin — How can we share DCS data : the Human Intracerebral EEG Platform (HIP)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25383/how-can-we-share-dcs-data-the-human-intracerebral-eeg-platform-hip-philippe-ryvlin); [Olivier Aron — Towards a probabilistic brain atlas of DCS responses](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25384/towards-a-probabilistic-brain-atlas-of-dcs-responses-olivier-aron). 

A research platform does not automatically determine the clinical report format, authorize data sharing, or supply a validated prediction. Those implementation and governance decisions remain separate from the scientific rationale.

## What to carry forward

A useful stimulation account allows another clinician to reconstruct what was asked, what was observed, and how the interpretation was reached. It can remain concise at the impression level while preserving rich evidence underneath. That is the bridge from an individual trial to the integrated reading discussed in Chapter 16.

## Sources and reading notes

The observation/impression structure is editorial synthesis. The cited primary studies illustrate why event definitions and individual anatomy matter. Conference platform/atlas material is presented as research direction, without adopting a data schema or sharing workflow. Existing local-template, confidence-scale, and conference-decision rows remain unchanged.

# Chapter 7 — Sensory and Perceptual Responses

## What the response can teach us

Sensory stimulation responses are experiments in perception. They show that perturbing a sampled site and its connections can alter an experience. Their teaching value lies in the combination of **phenomenology, anatomy, and experimental context**. “Auditory response” or “sensory aura” is a beginning, not an adequate account of the result.

The PAROS sensory talks describe regional stimulation anatomy in detail. Speaker interpretations and primary-study findings provide different levels of evidence for these associations. The regions producing a symptom during stimulation and the regions initiating a spontaneous seizure are related questions, but they are not identical experiments. 

## Auditory phenomena: an anatomical hierarchy with overlapping responses

### What was heard or changed?

An **auditory hallucination** is a sound experience without a corresponding external sound. Elementary experiences include buzzing, clicking, humming, or a tone; complex experiences include voices or music. An **auditory illusion** changes a sound that is actually present: its loudness, pitch, distance, clarity, or quality. Reduced hearing and an added sound are different effects, even when both are described as “my hearing changed.” These definitions organize Minotti's talk. [Lorella Minotti — Auditory symptoms](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25346/auditory-symptoms-lorella-minotti). 

Complexity is useful to describe, but it should not erase mixed responses. A patient may hear an elementary sound while speech in the room becomes muffled. A familiar voice can also acquire an experiential or emotional quality; the auditory and memory components can both be retained in the account. The distinction is between components of one experience, not necessarily separate events.

### Where have auditory responses been elicited?

Minotti emphasizes the supratemporal auditory system: **Heschl's gyrus and the superior temporal gyrus**, with surrounding auditory association cortex that includes the planum temporale and planum polare. Her review describes a concentration of elicited hallucinations in more medial auditory territories and a broader, more lateral/anterior distribution of illusions. This is an attributed summary of overlapping distributions, not a sharp boundary separating every simple from every complex experience. The transcript contains imperfect anatomical transcriptions; standard anatomical names are used here without reproducing those errors. 

Primary stimulation work helps make the distinction concrete. A three-patient study elicited hearing suppression from anterior lateral Heschl's gyrus and posterior lateral superior temporal cortex, with other auditory effects at some of the same locations. Thus, stimulating auditory cortex can add a percept or interfere with ongoing hearing; an anatomical discussion limited to positive hallucinations would be incomplete. [Hearing suppression study, 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3816378/).

Minotti also reviews **extratemporal** auditory responses, especially reports involving the posterior insula, and more scattered frontal or parietal sites. These observations introduce network participation and the importance of exact electrode anatomy. They do not justify moving every auditory symptom into a temporal-only category. 

### Why the insular attribution is disputed

An earlier insular stimulation cohort reported auditory responses. In Duong's later cohort, auditory effects occurred when the stimulated pairs involved adjacent superior temporal cortex on individual anatomical images. The authors explain that transformation into a standard brain could make those contacts appear insular. The disagreement concerns what tissue was stimulated, not whether the patient heard something. [Pugnaghi et al., 2011](https://air.unipr.it/handle/11381/3058105); [Duong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10893903/). 

For learning, retain both levels: posterior insular auditory responses are reported in the historical literature, and at least one carefully localized later study reassigned its apparent examples to neighboring auditory cortex. [Chapter 13](#chapter-13) develops the insular–opercular anatomy.

### Laterality is a feature of the experience

Minotti describes contralateral, bilateral, and occasional ipsilateral auditory experiences and cautions against a universal rule linking the perceived side of sound to the seizure-onset hemisphere. The distinction between hallucination and illusion also matters. A sound perceived in one ear, a sound located in one side of external space, and a bilateral change in hearing are not interchangeable descriptions. Her lateralization discussion is conference teaching; the transcript's pooled percentages have not been reproduced here because their denominators and classifications require checking against the underlying studies. 

## Smell and taste: neighboring experiences, overlapping representations

### Separate the sensory quality from its pleasantness

Olfaction concerns smell; gustation concerns taste. Everyday descriptions of “flavor” can combine taste, retronasal smell, and oral somatosensation. An unpleasant experience may be described as a bad smell, bitter taste, chemical sensation, or disgust. These descriptions should not be collapsed merely because all are aversive. Pana's talk explicitly distinguishes the modalities while discussing their anatomical overlap. [Raluca Pana — Gustatory & olfactory symptoms](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25347/gustatory-olfactory-symptoms-raluca-pana). 

Associated fear, autonomic sensations, oral movement, or salivation may add information about the full response. They do not establish that the sensory component arose from the same structure as every associated feature. The presence of a pleasant or unpleasant quality is likewise not an anatomical label.

### The insular finding deserves more than a passing mention

Pana emphasizes the **central/mid-dorsal insula** in gustatory stimulation responses. In Mazzola's primary study, taste responses occurred with stimulation of the posterior short gyrus, with overlap among gustatory, olfactory, and oral somatosensory representations in the mid-dorsal insula. This is a useful regional association and a reason to think about an integrated oral sensory representation. It is more informative than saying only that taste and smell “overlap.” [Mazzola et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28796326/). 

The **posterior short gyrus** is an insular gyral name. It should not be casually rewritten as “the whole posterior insula”: the short/long gyri, central sulcus, and anterior/posterior subdivisions are different anatomical descriptions. Broad labels can conceal precisely the detail a localization study contributes.

### Mesial temporal, orbitofrontal, and opercular observations

Pana places modern insular findings alongside older mesial temporal/uncal associations and reports involving the amygdala, orbitofrontal cortex, and opercula. Her review suggests that the distribution changes with which structures are implanted and how accurately contacts are assigned. Earlier studies with little insular coverage cannot estimate the insula's contribution in the same way as studies that sample it directly. This is a sampling explanation, not proof that every older temporal attribution was wrong. 

Fox's orbitofrontal cohort described smell, taste, and combined responses. A later orbitofrontal study by Pantis attributed many apparent olfactory effects to nearby non-orbitofrontal structures. As with the auditory-insular dispute, contact-level anatomy changes the interpretation of a regional label. The descriptive reports remain useful, while the source attribution requires qualification. [Fox et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6202946/); [Pantis et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40637402/). See [Chapter 14](#chapter-14). 

## Somatosensation, temperature, and pain

### Describe quality and distribution separately

A bodily response can be nonpainful/nonthermal, thermal, painful, or both painful and thermal. Tingling, numbness, pressure, electrical sensations, heat, and pain do not all carry the same phenomenology. The body distribution adds a second dimension: one digit, a limb segment, the face or mouth, a hemibody, or a diffuse sensation. A spreading distribution also differs from a fixed one. Colon emphasizes the limitations of studies that use broad labels without describing the patient's actual sensation. [Albert Colon — Somatosensory symptoms (dysesthesias, thermal sensations, pain)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25348/somatosensory-symptoms-dysesthesias-thermal-sensations-pain-albert-colon). 

These descriptions are not an instruction to provoke pain. They explain how to read a reported result obtained within an established clinical protocol.

### The regional landscape

Primary somatosensory cortex supplies the familiar somatotopic reference, but Colon's review extends well beyond S1. It includes S2/parietal operculum, insula, mesial parietal and cingulate regions, and more scattered reports from additional cortical and subcortical sites. The diversity of reported locations is itself a teaching point: a somatosensory response can reflect entry into a distributed system rather than a unique cortical address. 

The **posterior insular and opercular** literature is especially relevant for painful and thermal experiences. Duong's cohort separated painful/thermal responses from nonpainful/nonthermal somatosensory responses and found different, partly adjacent insular distributions. This supports retaining sensory quality when discussing anatomy rather than pooling every bodily response. [Duong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10893903/). 

Colon proposes a more specific pain-related location in the upper part of the anterior long gyrus of the posterior insula. He explicitly calls this his personal interpretation and says its foundation is insufficient. It is worth teaching as a **speaker hypothesis**, accompanied by that qualification. It should not be presented as an established single “pain center.” 

## Visual responses: what appears, what changes, and what fails

### A richer visual vocabulary

Positive phenomena include points or flashes of light, geometric forms, colors, motion, and formed images. An illusion or distortion alters something already being seen; a negative visual response is a loss or failure, such as a field defect or a task-related recognition problem. A failure to recognize a face, a change in the appearance of a face, and a complex visual hallucination are different observations.

Lee's 23-patient subdural study classified positive responses by **form, color, and motion**, with simple, intermediate, and complex forms. Its electrode modality and categories should remain explicit when applying its lessons to SEEG reading. [Lee et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10690975/). 

### Regional teaching from the visual talks

Martineau's mesial occipital review emphasizes elementary visual phenomena, including phosphenes, with differences among calcarine, lingual, and cuneal territories. Her synthesis describes predominantly elementary/static responses around calcarine cortex, a greater representation of color in lingual observations, and motion among cuneal experiences. Chipaux-Raffo discusses elementary as well as intermediate and complex responses in lateral occipital cortex, including reported negative visual symptoms. These are attributed distributions within the reviewed literature, whose classifications and sampling differ. [Laurence Martineau — Mesial occipital cortex](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25379/mesial-occipital-cortex-laurence-martineau); [Mathilde Chipaux-Raffo — Lateral occipital cortex](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25380/lateral-occipital-cortex-mathilde-chipaux-raffo). 

Maillard describes a **posterior-to-anterior gradient of visual complexity** across lingual and fusiform regions. Crucially, he also notes that elementary hallucinations can occur in anterior fusiform cortex. A gradient is a change in relative distribution, not a rule that a simple percept must arise posteriorly or a complex percept must arise anteriorly. His discussion of reading and face recognition adds task-specific failures to the positive visual phenomena. [Louis Maillard — Fusiform gyrus (T4) & lingual lobule (T5)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25368/fusiform-gyrus-t4-lingual-lobule-t5-louis-maillard). See [Chapters 12](#chapter-12) and [15](#chapter-15). 

## A worked reading example

*Hypothetical teaching example:* one stimulation produces a brief buzzing sound; another makes the examiner's voice sound distant. Calling both “auditory positive” hides the hallucination–illusion distinction. If the first contact is in Heschl's gyrus and the second near lateral superior temporal cortex, the observations can be discussed in relation to auditory organization. If either pair lies at an insular–temporal boundary, the contact anatomy becomes part of the explanation. Neither observation alone identifies where a spontaneous seizure began.

The same reasoning applies to a bitter taste versus an unpleasant smell, or a burning painful hand sensation versus painless tingling. Rich description makes anatomical teaching more precise. Anatomical detail, in turn, helps explain why the distinction was worth preserving.

## What to carry forward

The sensory talks provide regional associations, overlapping representations, and gradients of response—not just a list of labels. The most useful account preserves the experience, the tissue actually sampled, the EEG context, and the strength of the anatomical inference. Conflicting localization studies should be explained, not omitted. Speaker hypotheses can be retained when clearly identified as such.

Conference sources are the six linked PAROS talks; relevant transcript passages were checked, while audio/slides were not re-verified. The regional summaries are paraphrased teaching material. Mazzola, Lee, Pantis, and Pugnaghi are supported here at the checked abstract level; Duong's anatomical dispute and response distinctions follow the prior full-text source audit and current indexed primary passages. The hearing-suppression study provides a primary example of an auditory negative effect. Clinical adoption and operating procedures remain governed by the local protocol.

# Chapter 8 — Body, Self, Thought, and Memory Experiences

## An experiential response is not one phenomenon

A feeling that a hand has changed size, that the room is unreal, that a thought has been inserted, and that a present scene has happened before concern different parts of experience. Grouping all of them as “psychic” or “experiential” loses the content that makes them teachable.

Etholm, Laing, and Chatzikonstantinou/Aubert approach these phenomena from complementary directions: bodily representation, the sense of self and thought, and memory. Their talks also show why older terminology is difficult to map directly onto modern categories. A richly described case can support more precise interpretation than a larger collection of vaguely labeled experiences. 

## Bodily experience has several dimensions

Etholm distinguishes aspects of bodily self-consciousness that are often combined in ordinary language: where the self seems located, the first-person perspective, whether a body part feels one's own, whether an action feels self-generated, and how the body appears or feels. These are separable dimensions of description. [Lars Etholm — Bodily illusions (autoscopy, heautoscopy, OBE, levity)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25350/bodily-illusions-autoscopy-heautoscopy-obe-levity-lars-etholm). 

### Body-part changes

A limb may feel lighter, heavier, displaced, larger, smaller, absent, or moving when no corresponding movement is observed. A perceived movement therefore needs to be distinguished from an actual movement. Changes in body size or ownership are also more specific than a generic sensory disturbance.

Etholm's review includes more elaborate body-part phenomena, but their terminology is not uniform across reports. An unfamiliar limb sensation is not sufficient to establish a complex ownership syndrome. The actual description should lead the label, rather than the label supplying missing features.

### Whole-body and autoscopic experiences

A feeling of floating or whole-body displacement overlaps with vestibular experience. An out-of-body experience includes a displaced sense of self or viewing perspective, rather than merely dizziness or limb levity. Experiences of seeing one's own body, altered self-location, and uncertainty about the relation between the perceived and physical body can also differ. The historical terms autoscopy, heautoscopy, and out-of-body experience should not be treated as interchangeable without knowing which components were present. 

The educational question is therefore: did the experience concern a body part, whole-body position, visual duplication, ownership, agency, or viewpoint? This is an explanatory comparison, not a bedside diagnostic scale.

## Reported anatomy of bodily illusions

Etholm's stimulation synthesis emphasizes parietal and midcingulate sites, with neighboring cortical participation and additional posteromedial observations. He cautions that describing all adjoining regions as a single broad map can make the result anatomically uninformative. Both hemispheres appear in the literature; a simple unilateral rule would overstate the evidence. 

Three primary examples make the network argument concrete:

- **Angular gyrus:** Blanke's single-patient report repeatedly elicited an out-of-body experience with right angular stimulation, together with illusory limb transformations and whole-body displacement. The report supports a somatosensory–vestibular integration interpretation, not a universal right-angular diagnosis. [Blanke et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12239558/).
- **Cingulate cortex:** Popa's series described altered limb weight, movement, displacement, floating, and detachment among 17 stimulations in 12 patients, analyzed with connectivity. [Popa et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6865384/).
- **Anterior precuneus:** Lyu studied nine patients with posteromedial coverage and elicited changes in physical/spatial self-experience from specific anterior precuneus sites. Their identified region lay outside the default-mode network boundaries used in the study while remaining reciprocally connected with it. “Posteromedial” should therefore not automatically be translated into “within the default-mode network.” [Lyu et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37295420/). 

These experiments concern related but nonidentical experiences. They should be read as complementary observations about bodily integration, not as interchangeable replications of one symptom.

## Depersonalization and derealization

**Depersonalization-like experience** concerns detachment or unreality of the self or body. **Derealization-like experience** concerns the surroundings feeling unreal. Neither term alone diagnoses a psychiatric disorder. A transient stimulation-linked or ictal experience needs its neurological and experimental context.

Laing explains that early reports often mixed these descriptions with autoscopic, out-of-body, dreamlike, and mesial temporal phenomena. In his review, some more isolated depersonalization experiences involved posterior cingulate/posteromedial stimulation; derealization was also reported in insular material. He treats the historical variation in definitions as a major limitation. [Joshua Laing — Depersonalization, forced thinking, time illusion](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25351/depersonalization-forced-thinking-time-illusion-joshua-laing). 

Heydrich's study provides another useful distinction: it examined spontaneous ictal depersonalization- and derealization-like symptoms, not a standardized stimulation task. Its clinical definitions help clarify the words, but its regional associations cannot simply be transferred to a DCS result. [Heydrich et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6764488/). 

The Lyu findings above also caution against a broad network shortcut. Posterior cingulate and anterior precuneus are neighboring posteromedial territories with different evidence; they should not be merged solely because both can participate in altered self-experience.

## Forced thinking: an intrusive concept rather than a percept

Forced thought is described as involuntary, intrusive conceptual content. It differs from hearing spoken words, seeing an image, or recalling a particular episode. The patient may describe the content as alien, imposed, or difficult to disengage from, but the exact language varies.

Laing highlights prefrontal stimulation reports near the superior frontal sulcus and adjacent superior/middle frontal gyri. Liu's primary series describes three patients with left-dominant language and forced conceptual thought during left lateral prefrontal stimulation. Connectivity analyses related the sites to a wider frontal, temporal, and parietal network. Those analyses used independent imaging datasets; they were not a direct recording of every recruited node during each patient's thought. [Liu et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31951969/). 

The distinction from déjà vu is especially useful. An intrusive idea may feel repetitive or familiar, but that does not make it a familiarity illusion. A report of a concept, a percept, and a memory should be separable before regional interpretation is attempted. The source case narratives are not reproduced here.

## Altered time: retain the observation and the small evidence base

Laing found very little direct stimulation evidence specifically classified as time illusion. He discusses a single-case report of perceived slowing from stimulation involving insular/claustral and inferior frontal neighborhoods. This is a conference-attributed, sparsely supported observation with uncertain anatomical specificity; the underlying paper has not received a new full-text adjudication for this edition. 

It is still useful to teach that a perceived change in the passage of time can be described separately from confusion, amnesia, slowed speech, or a prolonged seizure. A patient's report that time slowed does not measure actual elapsed time or identify a unique timing center. The lack of a validated assessment in this manual is a reason to retain the uncertainty, not to erase the phenomenon.

## Memory: familiarity, recollection, and access to information

### Familiarity and recollection are different experiences

Familiarity is the sense that something has been encountered before, even when contextual detail is absent. Recollection contains details of an event or scene. The dysmnesia talk uses this distinction to organize familiarity illusions, semantic experiences, and episodic recollections. [Melina Symela Chatzikonstantinou & Sandrine Aubert — Dysmnesic symptoms (amnesia, (un)familiarity illusions, reminiscence)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25352/dysmnesic-symptoms-amnesia-unfamiliarity-illusions-reminiscence-melina-symela-chatzikonstantinou-sandrine-aubert). 

Déjà vu concerns inappropriate familiarity with the present situation. Déjà vécu and related historical labels may be used for richer experiences, but the level of detail must be checked in the report rather than inferred from the name. An unfamiliarity experience likewise deserves its own description: something known feels unfamiliar. These labels are descriptive terms whose usage varies across studies.

### The regional teaching

The speakers discuss a memory framework in which rhinal territories contribute to familiarity and hippocampal systems contribute to contextual recollection. Their stimulation review shows why this cannot be reduced to two isolated boxes. Lateral temporal responses are prominent in the mixed literature; depth-only analyses change the relative representation and highlight rhinal associations with familiarity. Amygdalar and hippocampal participation, and a small number of extratemporal reports, support a distributed interpretation. 

A change in findings after restricting to depth electrodes is methodologically important. It may reflect which structures were accessible and how experiences were classified. It should not be described as though surface and depth studies sampled the same anatomy with equal opportunity.

### Dream-related experiences

Curot distinguishes recollection of a specific dream, a vague sense of a familiar dream, and simply feeling as if dreaming. These differ from one another and from déjà vu. A vivid scene does not establish the accuracy of an autobiographical memory, and a dream-related experience is not automatically an ordinary episodic recollection. [Curot et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6028740/). 

### Amnesia requires evidence of a failure

Failure to form, retain, or retrieve information is different from a positive recollection or a familiarity illusion. A report that the patient “seemed confused” does not establish which memory process failed. The speakers include amnestic states in the broader dysmnesia landscape, but the sources checked here do not establish a comprehensive DCS amnesia test. Teaching the conceptual distinction is appropriate; assigning an unmeasured memory deficit is not. 

## A worked distinction

*Hypothetical teaching example:* one patient says the room feels unreal; another experiences a familiar scene without knowing why; a third describes an intrusive idea. All three may sound “dreamy” in a hurried summary. They differ in the object of the change: the surroundings, familiarity, or thought content. That difference determines which stimulation literature provides the most relevant comparison.

## What to carry forward

Experiential responses become more informative as their components are separated. Body representation, self-location, environmental reality, thought, familiarity, and recollection recruit overlapping systems but are not one phenomenon. Regional teaching should preserve both the anatomical findings and the methodological reasons they do not produce a unique symptom-to-site dictionary.

## Sources and reading notes

The three PAROS talks are the conference backbone. Blanke, Lyu, Liu, Popa, Heydrich, and Curot supply source-specific primary examples. Abstract-level checks are identified in the ledger; prior full-text audits remain the source for detailed Popa/Heydrich/Curot distinctions. The altered-time example remains conference-attributed. No patient narrative, cognitive battery, or local interpretation score is reproduced.

# Chapter 9 — Affective Experience and Emotional Behavior

## Emotion has subjective, bodily, and behavioral components

Fear, amusement, crying, laughter, a visceral sensation, and a measured autonomic change can occur together or separately. The report of a feeling is not interchangeable with its visible expression. Neither is identical to a physiological measurement.

The PAROS affective talks are especially useful because they describe both regional associations and the limits of a one-emotion/one-region model. Baumgartner reviews negative affect, Picard and Maliia review positive affect, and Ramantani examines affect-related behaviors. Their combined lesson is that detailed phenomenology and distributed anatomy belong together. 

## Fear and anxiety

Fear and anxiety are related but not uniformly defined in stimulation reports. Baumgartner discusses fear in relation to a threat and anxiety in relation to anticipated or less specific threat. Many historical papers did not document how the distinction was established. A modern synthesis should preserve the source's term and the patient's description instead of assuming all authors measured the same category. [Christoph Baumgartner — Negative affective symptoms (anger, anxiety, fear, sadness, guilt)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25354/negative-affective-symptoms-anger-anxiety-fear-sadness-guilt-christoph-baumgartner). 

### Regional associations worth learning

The amygdala and hippocampus are prominent in the reviewed fear literature; insular, cingulate, orbitofrontal, and other connected territories also appear. These observations support a limbic and distributed-network account. They do not justify labeling every amygdala response “fear,” or attributing every induced fear experience exclusively to the amygdala. 

A particularly important distinction is between a bodily threat response and consciously reported fear. Inman's amygdala stimulation study found physiological changes with comparatively infrequent subjective emotional reports. A change in autonomic physiology therefore need not be accompanied by the feeling the observer expects. [Inman et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6139084/). The article appeared online earlier; 2020 is its final issue year. 

The patient's fear of the procedure or of an impending seizure is also a different explanation from an internally elicited emotional state. The original report and temporal context matter when interpreting such a distinction; one cannot infer it merely from a facial expression.

## Sadness, anger, and guilt

Sadness should remain a description of the reported feeling. A transient stimulation-associated state labeled “depression” in a paper is not, by itself, a depressive disorder. Liu's 31-patient cohort separated fear, anxiety, happiness, and responses labeled depression; its network analysis concerned only three selected contacts in two patients. [Liu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12004395/). 

Baumgartner's lecture includes temporal and broader limbic associations for negative affect and a much smaller literature on anger, including an orbitofrontal example. He found very limited material classified as guilt. These differences in evidence volume should be visible. A sparsely described, personally meaningful experience interpreted as guilt does not establish a “guilt region.” 

The learning point is not to omit rare emotions, but to distinguish a repeatedly described response family from a few interpretively difficult reports. The certainty of the emotional label and the certainty of its anatomical attribution are separate issues.

## Mirth, smiling, and laughter

**Mirth** is experienced amusement. **Smiling and laughter** are behaviors. A person may laugh without reporting mirth, report mirth without laughing, or experience both. Caruana's cingulate series and Hao's separate emotional-stimulation series describe these dissociations. [Caruana et al., 2018](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/141/10/3035/5072015); [Hao et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10235553/). 

An absent record of mirth is not automatically a denial of mirth. This matters when reviewing older case reports, where the visible behavior may be described in detail but the subjective component was not elicited or recorded.

### Reported stimulation sites

Ramantani reviews laughter and smiling from several contexts, including anterior cingulate, frontal opercular and premotor territories, temporal/basal temporal regions, and hypothalamic hamartoma. The hamartoma literature is clinically familiar, particularly in pediatrics, but it is a distinct substrate and should not be pooled uncritically with cortical stimulation. [Georgia Ramantani — Affect-related behaviours (laughing, crying, pouting, biting, fighting, screaming, humming, kissing)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25356/affect-related-behaviours-laughing-crying-pouting-biting-fighting-screaming-humming-kissing-georgia-ramantani). 

A traditional teaching contrast assigns more motor-expression features to frontal/cingulate sites and more felt mirth to basal temporal sites. The stimulation literature contains exceptions, including mirthful cingulate responses. The contrast can be taught as a historical tendency while the exceptions prevent it from becoming a rigid anatomical rule.

Hao's seven-patient series excluded responses resembling habitual auras and those accompanied by afterdischarges or seizures. Its observations address selected physiological responses. They cannot be assumed to describe the same phenomenon as laughter occurring during an evolving induced seizure. 

## Ecstatic experience: more than ordinary happiness

Picard describes ecstatic experience using features such as exceptional mental clarity, certainty, intensified self-awareness, unity, and profound well-being. She distinguishes it from a modest pleasant feeling or calmness. These descriptions are valuable phenomenology; the new criteria proposed during the talk are not adopted here as a validated diagnostic checklist. [Fabienne Picard & Dragos-Mihai Maliia — Positive affective symptoms (mirth, ecstatic, mystic)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25355/positive-affective-symptoms-mirth-ecstatic-mystic-fabienne-picard-dragos-mihai-maliia). 

### The anterior-insular association

The talk emphasizes **anterior, often dorsal anterior, insular** stimulation in the ecstatic literature. This is a regionally informative association that should be preserved. It is also an example of the distinction between a symptom-producing region and the origin of a patient's spontaneous seizures.

A published report elicited clarity/bliss from dorsal anterior insular stimulation in a patient whose habitual epilepsy was not ecstatic. The spontaneous seizure and subsequent treatment evidence in that case supported a different seizure network. The observation demonstrates why an induced ecstatic experience does not automatically establish insular seizure onset. [Insular Stimulation Produces Mental Clarity and Bliss](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9300149/). 

Picard's mechanistic interpretation links the experience to altered processing of uncertainty and internal prediction errors. That is a hypothesis about the experience, not a proven cellular mechanism in every patient. The chapter retains it as speaker interpretation, separated from the directly elicited state.

### Mystical or religious meaning

A patient may interpret an experience of unity or revelation in mystical or religious terms. The patient's interpretation is part of the account, but it is not a separate anatomical diagnosis. The phenomenology can be described without endorsing or dismissing the meaning the patient gives it. The speaker explicitly discusses overlap between such interpretations and ecstatic experience. 

## Crying, sobbing, and pouting

Ramantani found far less stimulation evidence for crying, sobbing, and pouting than for laughter, and insufficient material to develop several other behaviors named in the original talk title. The chapter should reflect that unequal evidence base.

Her reviewed crying/sobbing material includes orbitofrontal observations; pouting is discussed in anterior cingulate and orbitofrontal contexts, with a limited primary literature. Facial expression, vocal sobbing, tears, and reported sadness are not interchangeable. Crying may have an emotional component, but that component needs to be described rather than assumed. [Georgia Ramantani — Affect-related behaviours (laughing, crying, pouting, biting, fighting, screaming, humming, kissing)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25356/affect-related-behaviours-laughing-crying-pouting-biting-fighting-screaming-humming-kissing-georgia-ramantani). 

A characteristic facial configuration historically called the *chapeau de gendarme* sign can be described as an observed expression. Its appearance alone does not supply a stimulation-based anatomical diagnosis. The spontaneous-seizure and directly induced behavior literatures need to remain distinguishable.

Biting, fighting, screaming, humming, and kissing should not receive equally confident regional entries merely because they appeared in the original session title. Ramantani explicitly narrowed her review to the behaviors for which she found usable data. Some vocal and oral phenomena are discussed further in [Chapter 10](#chapter-10).

## Emotion and the body

Soulier's cohort reports emotional sensations with and without associated bodily sensations. An abdominal feeling, throat sensation, or palpitation can accompany fear or another emotional state. The account becomes more informative when the feeling and bodily component remain separately identifiable. [Soulier et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9852394/). 

This also prevents circular interpretation. A visceral sensation from insular stimulation is not necessarily fear; fear accompanied by a visceral sensation is not necessarily a uniquely insular event. The regional chapters explain the broader networks.

## A worked reading example

*Hypothetical teaching example:* stimulation elicits a smile followed by laughter. The patient later reports surprise without amusement. A summary of “mirthful laughter” would overwrite the reported experience. A second patient reports intense amusement without visible laughter. These two observations test different assumptions about the relation between emotion and expression and should remain distinguishable in any anatomical comparison.

## What to carry forward

The affective literature teaches a network anatomy with useful regional concentrations, not a set of exclusive emotion centers. Preserve the experienced feeling, visible behavior, bodily accompaniment, and electrical context. Retain rare experiences with clear source attribution and proportional uncertainty rather than deleting them or giving them the same evidentiary weight as larger response families.

## Sources and reading notes

The PAROS negative-affect, positive-affect, and behavior talks supply the comprehensive teaching structure. Their regional syntheses and proposed mechanisms remain attributed. Primary studies cited here illustrate dissociation, selected-cohort limits, and anterior-insular ecstatic experience. No emotional-provocation procedure, psychiatric diagnosis, or local decision rule is introduced.

# Chapter 10 — Motor, Oral, Vocal, and Autonomic Manifestations

## Movement, intention, sensation, and physiology

A visible movement, an urge to move, the illusion of movement, and a measured physiological change are different observations. Their distinction is essential to the motor and autonomic chapters because the same broad label can hide different mechanisms and anatomical associations. A patient who feels the heart racing has reported an experience; an ECG-derived increase in heart rate is a measurement. Both matter, but neither silently substitutes for the other. 

## Elementary motor phenomena

A brief jerk, repeated rhythmic jerks, sustained contraction, and a patterned abnormal posture differ in their temporal and spatial organization. The familiar terms myoclonic, clonic, tonic, and dystonic help describe those differences, but an isolated movement should not be named solely from the anatomical region being stimulated. Its observed form, body distribution, and timing remain the starting point.

Cacic Hribljan and Dimova review the classic contrast between clonic/myoclonic responses associated with primary motor stimulation and tonic posturing associated with supplementary motor regions. They also emphasize motor responses from parietal and opercular territories. These additional sites make sense in a connected sensorimotor system and prevent a primary-motor-only account. [Melita Cacic Hribljan & Petia Dimova — Positive elementary motor signs (dystonic, tonic, myoclonic, myoclono-atonic, spasm)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25359/positive-elementary-motor-signs-dystonic-tonic-myoclonic-myoclono-atonic-spasm-melita-cacic-hribljan-petia-dimova). 

### Motor output and conscious movement experience can diverge

A person can experience apparent movement without visible movement, or move without a corresponding reported experience of initiating the action. The motor-sign talk uses this distinction to connect parietal representations with premotor output. It is a useful reason to preserve report and observation separately rather than assuming one from the other. [Chapter 8](#chapter-8) discusses the bodily-illusion side of this problem. 

### Myoclono-atonic phenomena and spasms

The speakers discuss these topics in relation to sensorimotor recruitment and inhibition, but the direct-stimulation evidence is uneven. They report difficulty finding clearly isolated spasms produced by stimulation outside a fuller seizure. That limited result should not be converted into either a universal absence claim or a validated stimulation marker for epileptic spasms. Physiological motor responses, negative motor effects, and ictal motor phenomena belong to related but distinct analyses. 

## Complex motor behavior and automatisms

Rheims distinguishes complex motor behavior from an elementary contraction, tonic/dystonic posture, or version. Organized responses may involve getting up, reaching, grasping, movements toward the body, or other coordinated actions. These can still be less elaborate than the behavior seen during a spontaneous seizure. [Sylvain Rheims — Motor automatisms (axial, distal, proximal, wandering)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25357/motor-automatisms-axial-distal-proximal-wandering-sylvain-rheims). 

### The cingulate association

The reviewed stimulation literature emphasizes the anterior portion of midcingulate cortex and related frontal motor networks. Caruana's large cingulate series describes goal-directed actions, impulses to get up, and other motor expressions. A reported urge is not identical to an executed action, and either can occur within a broader response. [Caruana et al., 2018](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/141/10/3035/5072015). 

Rheims highlights disagreement about posterior cingulate motor responses: one later report differs from studies that did not observe such behavior there. The disagreement should remain visible, rather than producing either a universal posterior-cingulate motor association or an absolute exclusion. [Chapter 15](#chapter-15) considers cingulate subregions.

## Oral movements, verbal output, and nonverbal vocalization

| Observation | Examples of what is being described | What the label does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Oral movement/automatism | Chewing, swallowing, lip or tongue movement. | A language error or a unique temporal origin. |
| Verbal automatism | Recognizable speech output during an induced event. | Preserved comprehension or a sole cortical generator. |
| Nonverbal vocalization | A sound without recognizable words. | The same mechanism as language production. |

Brazdil's review separates these families explicitly. It relates habitual oral automatisms in some reports to mesial temporal stimulation, while other oral responses involve frontal opercular or mesial frontal sites. Vocalization is repeatedly reported with SMA and ventral precentral stimulation. [Milan Brazdil — Oral, verbal, vocal automatisms](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25358/oral-verbal-vocal-automatisms-milan-brazdil). 

The most important qualification concerns verbal automatisms: several reports occurred with afterdischarges or induced seizures following amygdalar/hippocampal stimulation and subsequent spread to temporal neocortex or frontal operculum. Those observations support a network interpretation. They should not be retold as proof that stimulating the amygdala alone directly generated a particular language output.

Primary cingulate cohorts also distinguish lip/tongue movement, speech responses, motor urge, and other actions. Their categories are useful descriptive examples, not a complete universal motor taxonomy. [Rahman et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8909638/); [Xue et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10365235/). 

## Eyes and head: oscillation, blinking, and version

Chiarello distinguishes nystagmus, eye blinking, and sustained deviation of eyes or head. Nystagmus concerns oscillatory eye movement; sustained version is a different motor phenomenon. Direction, sequence, and whether the head follows the eyes all matter to the description. The transcript contains an inconsistent phrase about the phase defining nystagmus direction; that wording is not reproduced as a rule here. [Daniela Chiarello — Other positive elementary motor signs (epileptic nystagmus, eye blinking, gyration, version)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25360/other-positive-elementary-motor-signs-epileptic-nystagmus-eye-blinking-gyration-version-daniela-chiarello). 

Her review associates sustained contralateral eye deviation particularly with frontal eye-field stimulation, with supplementary eye-field observations that may include head turning and other motor or speech effects. Temporal and calcarine observations also appear in the much smaller nystagmus literature. This illustrates why an eye-movement label must specify the actual phenomenon before its anatomy is discussed.

The talk distinguishes isolated unilateral blinking reports in temporal contexts from rapid bilateral blinking in precentral contexts, but the small and heterogeneous stimulation record does not support a universal lateralizing rule. Whole-body turning, isolated eye deviation, and a combined eye–head sequence should not be collapsed into one “version” entry. 

## Gastrointestinal and visceral manifestations

Fernandez-Baca Vaca reviews abdominal sensations, nausea, vomiting, hypersalivation, and spitting, with much less evidence for other manifestations in the original title. Abdominal experiences dominate the selected stimulation literature; vomiting, salivation, and spitting are comparatively uncommon. [Guadalupe Fernandez-Baca Vaca — Gastrointestinal manifestations (painful and non painful sensations, flatulence, nausea, vomiting, hypersalivation, spitting)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25361/gastrointestinal-manifestations-painful-and-non-painful-sensations-flatulence-nausea-vomiting-hypersalivation-spitting-guadalupe-fernandez-baca-vaca). 

### Regional pattern

Insular and amygdalar sites are prominent, with hippocampal/parahippocampal, cingulate, and opercular observations also represented. The talk emphasizes how incomplete reporting of side, number of stimulations, and symptom subtype limits pooled conclusions. A report that many elicited abdominal sensations came from the insula is not the same as a probability that a new patient's abdominal aura originated there. 

Quality matters: rising epigastric sensation, pain, pressure, throat constriction, thoracic sensation, and nausea are not identical. Soulier's study separates viscero-sensitive and viscero-vegetative categories, but the latter includes subjective nausea or a feeling of tachycardia as well as potentially visible changes. It must not be translated into “objectively measured autonomic activity.” [Soulier et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9852394/). 

## Cardiovascular effects

Surges reviews heart-rate and blood-pressure changes across insular, amygdalar, cingulate, and other regions. He emphasizes that these are also responses to many emotional and physical challenges, so they have limited anatomical specificity when considered alone. [Rainer Surges — Cardiovascular manifestations (asystole, brady/tachycardia, hypo/hypertension)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25362/cardiovascular-manifestations-asystole-bradytachycardia-hypohypertension-rainer-surges). 

Chouchou's study analyzed ECG-derived responses to 100 insular stimulations in 47 patients. Both acceleration and slowing occurred with stimulation on either side. Within that cohort, acceleration was more posteriorly distributed and slowing more anterior/median. The changes were generally subtle and brief, and the selected population excluded important insular epileptic/pathological contexts. These findings provide regional physiology, not a simple “right speeds up, left slows down” rule. [Chouchou et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6865697/). 

Other studies reviewed by Surges differ in laterality and subregional findings. Blood pressure and heart rate are also different endpoints: one cannot infer that a measured change in one establishes the same change in the other. Neither endpoint alone defines a safety threshold or identifies a seizure-onset region.

## Skin, tears, and pupils: a small but informative literature

Rocamora describes a sparse stimulation literature with variable documentation. His review includes piloerection associated with amygdalar/parahippocampal stimulation in very few patients, pallor in mesial temporal contexts, sweating in cingulate/insular and some temporal-polar reports, and scattered pupillary and weeping observations. These are **conference-attributed, case-level regional observations**, not equally established entries in an autonomic atlas. [Rodrigo Rocamora — Cutaneous manifestations (flushing, pallor, piloerection, anhydrosis, sweating), weeping & pupillary changes](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25363/cutaneous-manifestations-flushing-pallor-piloerection-anhydrosis-sweating-weeping-pupillary-changes-rodrigo-rocamora). 

The visible sign and subjective account can diverge. A patient may not see their own pallor, flushing, or piloerection. Tears do not by themselves establish sadness, and a crying sensation does not prove visible lacrimation. Pupil change requires an observation or measurement, rather than an inference from visual discomfort.

Rocamora's material also demonstrates why detailed individual reports can be valuable: a well-localized observation with its electrical context may be more interpretable than a pooled category with missing anatomy. That does not convert a single report into a reliable localizer.

## A worked comparison

*Hypothetical teaching example:* a trial produces an abdominal rising sensation and the patient says the heart is racing. If there is no corresponding physiological measurement, the account contains two reported experiences. In another trial, the ECG shows a heart-rate change without a reported sensation. These findings can be discussed together, but they should not be counted as identical “autonomic responses.”

## What to carry forward

Motor and autonomic teaching becomes richer when movement, urge, perception, expression, and measured physiology remain distinguishable. There are useful regional concentrations—sensorimotor, mesial frontal/cingulate, mesial temporal, insular, and opercular—but their meaning depends on response type, electrical context, and sampling. Rare signs deserve proportional detail and explicit source attribution.

## Sources and reading notes

The seven PAROS talks provide comprehensive coverage. The primary cohorts support selected cingulate, visceral, and cardiac observations. Unpublished speaker observations, small case series, and broad seizure-semiology comparisons are not treated as independent validation. The approved local protocol governs monitoring, stopping, and treatment; this chapter provides no new intervention threshold.

# Chapter 11 — Language, Praxis, and Higher-Order Function

## Language is a set of operations, not an on/off response

Tassi's central teaching is that stimulation-induced language changes form a spectrum. A patient may pause, substitute a sound, produce the wrong word, struggle to retrieve a name, fail to read, or lose comprehension of particular material. Labeling every result “speech arrest” or “language positive” removes the distinctions needed to understand the functional anatomy. [Laura Tassi — Dysphasic and dyspraxic symptoms](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25353/dysphasic-and-dyspraxic-symptoms-laura-tassi). 

The task makes the observation interpretable. The same silence during picture naming, repetition, spontaneous speech, or a nonverbal task can have different implications. The relevant comparison is the patient's performance at baseline under the assessment actually used, not resemblance to their seizures.

## Decompose the task before interpreting the error

Picture naming requires access to the visual stimulus, recognition of the depicted object, semantic knowledge, retrieval of a lexical form, phonological organization, and speech production. Reading and auditory comprehension place different demands on that system. A response may affect one component while others remain available.

Tassi argues against using the same simple picture-naming task as though it adequately tests every sampled cortical region. Her examples emphasize reading, lexical/semantic functions, expression, and comprehension. This is the rationale for a considered language assessment; it does not prescribe a battery for an individual patient. 

## A vocabulary of errors

| Description | What the observation concerns | What needs clarification |
|---|---|---|
| Anomia/naming failure | Difficulty producing the intended name. | Was the item recognized, and could the task be performed at baseline? |
| Phonological/phonemic error | Alteration or substitution of speech sounds. | How did the produced form differ from the intended word? |
| Semantic error | A word related in meaning, or a disturbance involving meaning. | Was this a lexical substitution, recognition problem, or broader comprehension difficulty? |
| Output interruption | Reduced or absent speech during the task. | Were articulation, language formulation, attention, or another behavior affected? |
| Comprehension difficulty | Failure to understand the tested material. | What material and response demonstrated that failure? |
| Reading, repetition, or writing error | A change within that specific activity. | Which component changed, and what other functions were actually assessed? |

These are explanatory terms, not mutually exclusive diagnoses. The language literature uses varying error definitions, and identical labels do not ensure identical assessments. [Aron et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7987679/); [Cockle et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12514387/). 

## Three broad anatomical territories, with individual organization

Tassi organizes much of the literature around **anterior language**, **posterior language**, and **basal temporal language** regions. That is a useful orientation, provided it is not treated as three sharply bounded and interchangeable targets. The actual sampled subregion and task differ among studies. 

### Anterior perisylvian and frontal contributions

Inferior frontal and adjacent opercular/premotor territories participate in language and speech-related processes. Their stimulation may interfere with output, articulation, or other task-dependent operations. A motor-speech interruption near ventral sensorimotor cortex is not automatically equivalent to a lexical or semantic disturbance from another frontal site. [Chapter 14](#chapter-14) describes the frontal regional teaching.

### Posterior temporal and temporoparietal contributions

Posterior temporal and inferior parietal regions participate in auditory–language, phonological, semantic, and multimodal processes, depending on the particular territory and task. A broad “posterior language” label does not identify which of those demands was tested. The lateral temporal and parietal discussions in [Chapters 12](#chapter-12) and [15](#chapter-15) help relate error type to sampled anatomy. 

### Basal temporal contributions

The fusiform/ventral temporal literature includes naming and reading disturbances. Maillard's talk describes a posterior-to-anterior progression from more visual operations toward lexical-semantic access, with overlap rather than a perfectly separated sequence. Naming a visually presented object, recognizing a face, and reading a word are different tasks in neighboring ventral-stream systems. [Louis Maillard — Fusiform gyrus (T4) & lingual lobule (T5)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25368/fusiform-gyrus-t4-lingual-lobule-t5-louis-maillard). 

These associations are substantive anatomy for learning. They do not establish that every patient has the same distribution, that one task tests every contribution, or that a regional name defines a surgical margin.

## Dominance is not a synonym for left

Tassi explicitly distinguishes dominant from nondominant hemisphere rather than assuming that those terms always mean left and right. Epilepsy, developmental history, and individual organization make patient-specific interpretation necessary. Functional imaging can contribute to hypotheses and sampling, but its activation map and the effects of direct stimulation are different measurements. [Laura Tassi — Dysphasic and dyspraxic symptoms](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25353/dysphasic-and-dyspraxic-symptoms-laura-tassi). 

A stimulated site with a task-linked effect is patient-level functional evidence. It does not, by itself, describe the entirety of language dominance or identify every region necessary for preserved communication.

## What makes a language response difficult to interpret?

Baseline errors, task ambiguity, inconsistent engagement, and an accompanying discharge can all alter the interpretation. Young's 15-adult comparison study did not count a language disturbance occurring during a seizure as a language-mapping hit. That was an explicit methodological choice, illustrating why a study's endpoint must be known before its results are generalized. [Young et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8252889/). 

Likewise, repeated errors may increase the evidence that a change is linked to the experimental condition, but the required consistency and positive-site criteria are not uniform across publications. “Reproducible” should tell the reader what repeated, not imply that an unstated universal rule was satisfied.

## Intermediate-frequency stimulation: why the question is interesting

Moreno's short communication explores stimulation between the traditional low- and high-frequency approaches, describing a longer opportunity to examine different language components and fewer afterdischarges in his preliminary experience. He also calls for further research and standardization. Those are preliminary speaker findings, not an established replacement protocol. [Julio Moreno — Intermediate DCS for language mapping](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25390/intermediate-dcs-for-language-mapping-julio-moreno). 

A 23-patient comparative study is part of the existing source trail. The paper's specific method and endpoints matter; the phrase “intermediate frequency” is not itself a standardized exposure. [Abarrategui et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38581367/). The educational value is to understand why train duration, electrical interference, and task demands interact. No setting or method change is prescribed here. 

## Praxis and speech-motor function

Praxis concerns skilled learned actions. Apraxia of speech concerns planning/programming speech movements and should not be used as proof that limb praxis or a broader learned-action system has been tested. Weakness, sensory change, language misunderstanding, or an inability to engage with the task can also complicate interpretation of an apparent action failure.

The current source trail is stronger for language and speech-motor phenomena than for a general DCS praxis assessment. This is a real content boundary, but it can be explained: a chapter title mentioning dyspraxia does not supply an examination or a validated classification. The conceptual distinction is retained while the missing evidence is made specific. 

## Beyond language

Attention, memory, visuospatial processing, executive function, and social-emotional operations are relevant to cognitive mapping. They require tasks that engage the process of interest and a baseline that makes a change detectable. A cortical region that produces no effect during picture naming has not thereby passed a test of all these functions.

The broader mapping literature discusses active stimulation, passive recording of task-related activity, and connectivity measurements. Each asks a different question: the effect of perturbation, activity associated with a task, or a relationship among sites. Convergence can be informative, while disagreement can reveal task or method differences. [Drane et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8072290/). 

## A worked task comparison

*Hypothetical teaching example:* a patient cannot produce an object's name during stimulation but can give an appropriate description of its use. That observation differs from inability to recognize the object at all. A second patient produces a distorted speech-sound sequence while apparently retaining the intended concept. The precise errors make the anatomical discussion more useful than the shared label “naming positive.” These examples illustrate reasoning and do not select a clinical battery.

## What to carry forward

Language mapping teaches most when errors are tied to tasks and anatomy. Retain the anterior/posterior/basal temporal organization as a framework, then add patient-specific sampling, error type, and electrical context. A rich description makes the finding more interpretable; it does not require pretending that language is a single center or that a negative task result proves absence of function.

## Sources and reading notes

Tassi provides the conference backbone, Maillard the ventral temporal extension, and Moreno the preliminary methods discussion. The linked language studies and reviews supply task and endpoint context. Praxis and higher-order testing gaps are scoped to this manual's checked sources. Actual task selection, multilingual/pediatric adaptation, clinical positive-site decisions, and surgical use remain part of the reviewed local process.

# Chapter 12 — Temporal Regions: Mesial, Lateral, and Ventral Systems

## A temporal response needs a more precise address

The temporal lobe contains auditory, language, visual, memory, affective, and autonomic systems. “Temporal stimulation” is therefore too broad to explain a finding. The amygdala, hippocampal formation, rhinal cortices, temporal pole, lateral temporal gyri, and ventral temporal cortex differ in anatomy and in the experiments used to study them.

The regional talks provide a useful framework: identify the structure, describe its connections and reported effects, then ask how much the particular study actually sampled. This chapter retains that anatomical detail while distinguishing subjective experiences from measured physiology and task performance. 

## Amygdala: heterogeneous tissue within a connected system

Rubio-Agusti describes the amygdala as a collection of nuclei with different connections, rather than a single fear-producing structure. Broadly, cortical/superficial components are related to olfactory processing, basolateral territories receive extensive cortical information, and more medial output-related territories connect with hypothalamic/brainstem systems. He emphasizes that clinical electrodes often sample the large basolateral complex more readily than the smaller neighboring nuclei. [Ignacio Rubio-Agusti — Amygdala](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25364/amygdala-ignacio-rubio-agusti). 

The implication is important: a response labeled “amygdalar” does not establish equal sampling of every nucleus. Nor does a basolateral label prove functional uniformity within that complex. Differences in nuclear parcellation across papers further limit exact comparisons.

### Reported responses

The talk reviews emotional, respiratory/autonomic, olfactory/gustatory, memory-like, and visual/experiential effects, often in combinations. An odor linked to a memory and an emotion is not three independent proofs of three separate generators; it may reveal the integration of sensory information, memory, and affect within a connected system. 

Primary cohorts illustrate different approaches. Inman used controlled physiological measurements and found autonomic changes with relatively uncommon subjective emotion. Zhang's 48-person SEEG cohort described a broader set of reported experiences and subnuclear distributions, with uneven coverage and limited quantitative autonomic/emotional measurement. The studies ask related but different questions. [Inman et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6139084/); [Zhang et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10203795/). 

### Respiratory findings deserve explicit teaching

Amygdala stimulation and seizure spread into the amygdala have been associated with breathing inhibition in human intracranial research. Some patients did not report awareness of the respiratory interruption. The direct study by Dlouhy provides a primary example of this dissociation. [Dlouhy et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26180203/). 

This is a significant physiological observation: subjective comfort is not equivalent to an absence of respiratory effect. It is not a proposal to reproduce apnea or a substitute for the unit's established respiratory monitoring and safety procedures. Rubio-Agusti also discusses method- and subregion-dependent respiratory responses; the transcript does not justify one universal respiratory effect for all amygdala stimulation.

## Hippocampus and rhinal cortex

### Anatomical relationships

Fahoum describes the perirhinal, entorhinal, parahippocampal, and hippocampal systems as an interconnected route between neocortical information and hippocampal processing. The entorhinal region is a major interface with the hippocampal formation. “Rhinal cortex” is a useful collective term in some reports, but it should not erase the distinction between entorhinal and perirhinal sampling. [Firas Fahoum — Hippocampus & rhinal cortex (entorhinal and perirhinal)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25365/hippocampus-rhinal-cortex-entorhinal-and-perirhinal-firas-fahoum). 

The precision of the anatomical claim should match the data. A paper that identifies anterior versus posterior hippocampal contacts cannot necessarily distinguish hippocampal subfields. Likewise, a broad rhinal label may be insufficient for a claim about a particular cortical subdivision.

### Positive memory experiences

Déjà vu, familiarity, recollection, dreamlike states, and an induced familiar aura are different phenomena. Fahoum discusses interactions among rhinal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala during some experiences. The dysmnesia talk similarly emphasizes networks and the way depth-only versus mixed-electrode analyses change the regional distribution. [Melina Symela Chatzikonstantinou & Sandrine Aubert — Dysmnesic symptoms (amnesia, (un)familiarity illusions, reminiscence)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25352/dysmnesic-symptoms-amnesia-unfamiliarity-illusions-reminiscence-melina-symela-chatzikonstantinou-sandrine-aubert). 

Bancaud's temporal series described dreamy states with stimulation involving temporal neocortex, anterior hippocampus, or amygdala, often accompanied by spread between medial and lateral temporal structures. Those observations are informative precisely because the wider network was involved. [Bancaud et al., 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8149215/). 

### No overt experience does not mean no memory effect

Fahoum notes that hippocampal stimulation may produce no obvious subjective response when specific cognitive functions are not tested. An experiment without a memory task cannot establish that memory was unchanged. Positive recollection and a change in the ability to learn are separate endpoints.

Two studies show why method and task matter. Suthana's seven-participant study found improved subsequent spatial memory for locations learned during entorhinal-region stimulation under its experimental conditions; direct hippocampal stimulation did not show the same benefit. Jacobs later found impaired spatial and verbal episodic performance during entorhinal-region/hippocampal stimulation in different experiments. [Suthana et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22316444/); [Jacobs et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27930911/). 

These findings should be taught together. They do not support the slogans “entorhinal stimulation enhances memory” or “all mesial temporal stimulation impairs memory.” They require comparison of location, task, timing, stimulation method, and electrical context. Fahoum stresses afterdischarge context, but that factor alone should not be claimed to explain all differences between the studies.

## Temporal pole: a multimodal region with adjacent-structure confounds

Novitskaya describes a heterogeneous stimulation literature with sensory, vestibular, experiential, and other responses. Many earlier reports provide an anatomical name without coordinates, and relatively few patients in a larger temporal study may contribute temporal-pole findings. Those denominators matter when assessing how representative a response is. [Yulia Novitskaya — Temporal pole](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25366/temporal-pole-yulia-novitskaya). 

Painful facial, palatal, dental, or head sensations are especially instructive. The talk discusses possible current spread near the skull base and activation of nearby pathways as an explanation for some findings. This is a proposed confound, not proof that all pain near the temporal pole arises by one mechanism.

The later 50-patient temporal-pole cohort similarly reports broad responses and limited localizing value, with skull-base spread explicitly discussed. [Novitskaya and Schulze-Bonhage, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13240784/). Earlier work also reports heterogeneous limbic/paralimbic effects and frequent afterdischarge association. [Ostrowsky et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12424087/). 

The teaching point is substantive: unexpected pain or movement during temporal-pole stimulation should not automatically be interpreted as a newly discovered intrinsic temporal-pole function.

## Lateral temporal cortex: superior, middle, and inferior territories

Cloppenborg reviews the superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyri. Auditory responses concentrate in superior temporal territories, language findings depend strongly on task and hemisphere, and visual phenomena appear in posterior/inferior lateral temporal regions. The borders with temporal pole, parietal association cortex, and occipital regions affect how studies classify the same observation. [Thomas Cloppenborg — Lateral temporal cortex (T1-T2-T3)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25367/lateral-temporal-cortex-t1-t2-t3-thomas-cloppenborg). 

His discussion includes task-related spatial distinctions, such as differing auditory versus visual naming distributions in selected studies. These should not be simplified into a single “naming area”: auditory naming and visual naming start with different inputs and share only some operations. Nakai's large mapping cohort likewise illustrates age-, task-, and hemisphere-related differences, under its particular electrode method. [Nakai et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5405238/). 

## Ventral temporal cortex: visual content, faces, and words

### Lingual and fusiform anatomy

Maillard distinguishes lingual from fusiform territories and emphasizes their relation to neighboring parahippocampal/rhinal cortex and the collateral sulcus. Studies that combine them under “ventral temporal cortex” answer a broader question than studies that localize contacts to particular gyri. [Louis Maillard — Fusiform gyrus (T4) & lingual lobule (T5)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25368/fusiform-gyrus-t4-lingual-lobule-t5-louis-maillard). 

### Gradients with overlap

His teaching describes a posterior-to-anterior increase in the complexity of some evoked visual experiences and task effects. The gradient is probabilistic: elementary visual hallucinations can still occur in anterior fusiform cortex. Laterality also depends on the response being examined, rather than following one rule for every ventral temporal function.

Face-related changes, impaired face recognition, reading disruption, and naming errors are distinct outcomes. Maillard discusses right-sided face-related findings and left-sided language/reading contributions, while emphasizing the need to know the precise task and site. These are regional teaching tendencies, not an assignment of all face function to one hemisphere or all language to the other. 

A single SEEG case elicited a scene hallucination from a place-selective collateral-sulcus/medial-fusiform site. Another study reported naming disruption with middle fusiform stimulation. The two results demonstrate why internally generated visual content and failure on a naming task should not share a generic “visual positive” label. [Mégevand et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6608225/); [Forseth et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6365955/). 

Maillard also questions whether some reports of fusiform-elicited dreamy states include neighboring rhinal tissue. That is his interpretation of an anatomical discrepancy, not a resolved correction of the other study. Retaining the disagreement helps readers understand why exact sulcal boundaries matter.

## What to carry forward

Temporal stimulation spans perception, memory, language, emotion, and physiology. Identify the subregion and the endpoint before interpreting the response. Controlled task effects, subjective experiences, physiological changes, and induced seizure fragments teach different lessons. The most informative synthesis preserves those distinctions and the networks linking them.

## Sources and reading notes

The five linked regional talks are the backbone. The primary studies are deliberately contrasted rather than treated as interchangeable replications. Exact nuclear/subfield maps and a patient-level resection boundary are not inferred. Primary abstracts and previously audited full-text passages support the stated examples; conference mechanisms and anatomical explanations remain attributed.

# Chapter 13 — Insular, Opercular, and Cingulo-Opercular Networks

## A deep region and its covering cortex

The insula lies beneath the opercula. Frontal, rolandic/parietal, and temporal opercular territories are not interchangeable surfaces, and a depth trajectory may encounter both opercular and insular tissue. A stimulated pair can therefore be wholly within one territory or span an anatomical boundary. That geometry matters before a response is called “insular.” 

The chapter brings together subjective sensations, motor/speech effects, measured autonomic physiology, connectivity experiments, and ictal studies. They describe complementary aspects of the system rather than one composite response test.

## Insular organization: anatomy before shorthand

The central insular sulcus separates the anterior short-gyrus region from the posterior long-gyrus region. Individual gyri, anterior/posterior position, dorsal/ventral location, and microscopic parcellations are different ways of describing anatomy. A finding assigned to one of these systems should not be transferred to another merely because the labels sound similar. 

For example, the posterior short gyrus and the posterior insula are not synonymous. The gustatory study discussed in Chapter 7 localizes responses to the posterior short gyrus/mid-dorsal region. Preserving that wording conveys more than the broad label “posterior.” [Mazzola et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28796326/).

## Regional response families

| Reported emphasis | Examples of response content | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Posterior/sensorimotor-related insular territories | Nonpainful somatic, thermal, painful, and some vestibular experiences. | The distributions overlap and depend on the response definition and anatomy. |
| Central/mid-dorsal insular territories | Gustatory/olfactory and oral sensory overlap in selected studies. | Taste, smell, and oral sensation should remain separate observations. |
| More anterior/interoceptive-affective territories | Visceral sensations, anxiety/foreboding, and selected ecstatic experiences. | A subjective feeling, measured physiology, and task function are different endpoints. |
| Insular boundary with temporal cortex | Historical auditory reports and disputed anatomical attribution. | Individual contact localization may reveal adjacent superior temporal involvement. |

This table summarizes source-specific teaching rather than expected responses at each contact. The conference symptom talks and the primary insular studies supply the underlying observations. [Raluca Pana — Gustatory & olfactory symptoms](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25347/gustatory-olfactory-symptoms-raluca-pana); [Albert Colon — Somatosensory symptoms (dysesthesias, thermal sensations, pain)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25348/somatosensory-symptoms-dysesthesias-thermal-sensations-pain-albert-colon); [Fabienne Picard & Dragos-Mihai Maliia — Positive affective symptoms (mirth, ecstatic, mystic)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25355/positive-affective-symptoms-mirth-ecstatic-mystic-fabienne-picard-dragos-mihai-maliia); [Duong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10893903/). 

Duong's 17-person study examined pairs within the individual insular boundary and found reportable experiences at a subset of them. Its fine-grained analysis separated pain/temperature, nonpainful somatic, and visceral responses. The study also illustrates a crucial limitation: absence of a reportable experience is not equivalent to absence of physiological function, because the subjective stimulation assay and task-related recordings measure different things.

## The auditory boundary problem

Older insular series reported auditory phenomena. In Duong's study, the pairs producing auditory effects involved adjacent superior temporal cortex outside the native-space insular boundary. Standard-space transformation could have altered that assignment. The patient's reported experience is not in dispute; the tissue attribution is.

This is a generalizable anatomical lesson: a regional atlas label, a normalized coordinate, and the individual position of both contacts need not convey identical information. A response should be discussed at the resolution the anatomy supports. 

## Opercular subdivisions and their effects

Mălîia's 31-person opercular cohort describes different distributions of responses across frontal, rolandic, parietal, and temporal opercula, with connectivity examined separately in a subset. Speech/language, auditory, somatosensory, motor, and other effects cannot be adequately represented by one “opercular response” category. [Mălîia et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30414541/). 

Della Santa's short communication compares insular and opercular observations. In that preliminary analysis, somatosensory/interoceptive/gustatory experiences were relatively more prominent with insular stimulation, while speech/language and auditory effects were relatively more prominent in opercular observations. The comparison is informative as a research result; it does not establish mutually exclusive functions. [Bianca Della Santa — The organisation and balance of insular vs opercular regions through the lens of direct electrical stimulation during SEEG](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25389/the-organisation-and-balance-of-insular-vs-opercular-regions-through-the-lens-of-direct-electrical-stimulation-during-seeg-bianca-della-santa). 

### Mixed pairs are a scientific question

A pair spanning insula and operculum can produce an effect that resembles one side's response profile. Della Santa proposes studying these mixed stimulations as a way to understand interaction between the two structures, rather than simply discarding them from every research analysis.

This does not establish that a clinician can assign a mixed response to one contact or calculate each structure's contribution. The current field, local anatomy, and recruited connections remain unresolved parts of the experiment. A hypothesis about interaction should not be rewritten as a contact-selection rule. 

### The proposed somatotopic continuum

The same communication describes a preliminary posterior-to-anterior ordering from limb-related toward oral, throat, and visceral experiences, and proposes a continuum linking external and internal body representations. The proposed “inside-out” homunculus is a speaker model. It can help readers understand the research question, but it is not a validated insular atlas or a universal phylogenetic explanation. 

## Cardiac physiology and interoception

A measured heart-rate change differs from feeling a palpitation, and both differ from interpreting an emotion. Chouchou's cohort found both slowing and acceleration after stimulation on either side of the insula, with subregional differences within the cohort. That result resists a simple hemispheric sympathetic/parasympathetic split. [Chouchou et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6865697/). 

The broader cardiovascular talk includes amygdalar and cingulate observations as well as insular effects. Thus, an insular contribution to cardiac control does not make every autonomic seizure manifestation uniquely insular. [Chapter 10](#chapter-10) discusses the measurement and laterality issues.

## Connectivity is not the same as seizure propagation

A cortico-cortical evoked potential records a response at another sampled site after stimulation. It provides evidence about effective connectivity under that paradigm. Dionisio's 30-person study found distributed insular connectivity patterns differing by sampled gyrus and explicitly considered ambiguous insular/opercular anatomy. [Dionisio et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6825888/). 

An ictal study asks a different question: where spontaneous seizure activity appears and how it evolves. Singh's selected insular-onset cohort described heterogeneous intrainsular dynamics and early extrainsular spread, with much clinical semiology emerging during propagation. [Singh et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32542728/). A connection demonstrated with a pulse is not proof that a particular seizure traveled along that connection.

## Cingulo-opercular-insular overlap

Oane's communication presents a research proposal motivated by early recruitment of insular/opercular and cingulate regions in some seizures. Her examples show how overlapping semiology and connected activation can accompany different patient-specific seizure hypotheses. The proposed analysis seeks to compare clinical sequence and signal features, rather than assuming that one shared symptom identifies the origin. [Irina Oane — Seizure semiology of the cingulo–opercular-insular network: Electroclinical implications for presurgical evaluation](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25392/seizure-semiology-of-the-cingulo-opercular-insular-network-electroclinical-implications-for-presurgical-evaluation-irina-oane). 

The communication is not a completed validation of an integration algorithm. Its value here is the conceptual problem: adjacent and strongly connected regions can participate early, and similar later behavior can arise from different starting points. The symptom chapters provide the descriptive vocabulary; the ictal recording provides the temporal evidence.

## What to carry forward

Insular anatomy should be taught with its gyri, boundaries, and neighboring opercula intact. There are meaningful response distributions, but the strength of an inference depends on exact sampling and what was measured. Mixed stimulation, connectivity, and seizure propagation are productive research questions rather than reasons to erase regional detail.

## Sources and reading notes

The primary cohorts support specific insular, opercular, cardiac and connectivity observations. Della Santa's mixed-pair and somatotopic models remain preliminary conference teaching; Oane's integration project remains a proposal. The reading edition does not turn them into a contact map, diagnostic sequence, or surgical rule.

# Chapter 14 — Frontal and Motor Regions: From Experience to Action

The frontal lobe is a particularly good place to learn why the response to stimulation depends on both anatomy and the question asked. A contraction, an urge to move, an interruption of movement, a naming error, and a change in emotion belong to different levels of description. An apparently quiet stimulation may simply have occurred while the patient was not performing the function of interest.

This chapter moves from orbitofrontal and prefrontal cortex toward premotor and primary motor regions. It combines the anatomical teaching in the PAROS regional talks with the studies identified below; the organization is an educational synthesis rather than a validated functional atlas. 

## Orbitofrontal and frontopolar cortex

### Anatomy and proposed functions

The orbitofrontal cortex occupies the ventral frontal surface. In Mindruta's PAROS teaching, the orbital sulci divide this surface into medial, lateral, anterior, and posterior territories; the frontopolar region lies farther anteriorly. Brodmann labels are useful orientation terms, but sulcal anatomy and modern parcellations do not correspond perfectly to a short list of numbers. In particular, medial orbital, posterior orbital, and frontal-polar contacts should not all be reported simply as “OFC.” [Ioana Mindruta — Orbito-frontal (BA 11-12-13-14) and fronto-polar (BA10) cortex](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25369/orbito-frontal-ba-11-12-13-14-and-fronto-polar-ba10-cortex-ioana-mindruta)

The talk places these territories within networks for reward evaluation, sensory value, emotional regulation, and autonomic experience. Olfactory, gustatory, visual, somatosensory, and auditory information can contribute to those networks. This gives a reason to consider the *meaning and value* of a percept alongside its sensory quality. It does not establish that stimulation of a particular orbital gyrus should reliably reproduce an odor, taste, or emotion. 

### Why the stimulation literature disagrees

Fox and colleagues reported affective, olfactory, gustatory, and somatosensory experiences in a 22-person orbitofrontal stimulation study. Their findings encouraged a view of orbital cortex as a site where multimodal and affective experiences can be elicited. A later report by Pantis and colleagues, involving 49 participants, found that almost all quality-controlled orbitofrontal sites produced no reportable subjective effect. Apparent olfactory or visual effects were often reassigned to nearby non-orbitofrontal structures, and the earlier organization by valence was not reproduced. [Fox et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6202946/); [Pantis et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40637402/).

The practical lesson is anatomical: knowing the electrode trajectory is not equivalent to knowing which tissue accounts for the effect. A pair labelled “orbitofrontal” may include a boundary or nearby structure relevant to the response. The two reports also arise from a shared investigative program; participant overlap has not been resolved in this manual. They should not be presented as independent positive and negative replications with otherwise identical methods. Nor does a lack of an immediate subjective effect test the full range of value-based decisions or social behavior. 

## Ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and the inferior frontal gyrus

Ostrowsky-Coste's regional talk distinguishes pars opercularis, pars triangularis, and pars orbitalis, approximately associated with areas 44, 45, and 47. Immediately posterior, the ventral precentral region contributes prominently to articulation and motor phenomena. This is more informative than using “Broca's area” for every inferior frontal speech-related effect. [Karine Ostrowsky-Coste — Prefrontal cortex : ventrolateral cortex (BA44-45-47)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25370/prefrontal-cortex-ventrolateral-cortex-ba44-45-47-karine-ostrowsky-coste)

The conference synthesis describes an anterior–posterior gradient: posterior sites more often produce articulatory or motor disturbances, while additional language tasks expose disturbances beyond simple speech output. A patient who cannot produce a spoken answer may have impaired articulation, impaired selection of a word, disrupted phonological processing, or difficulty maintaining the task. The stopped answer alone does not choose among these explanations. [Chapter 11](#chapter-11) develops the task distinctions.

The same anatomical neighborhood can produce non-language effects. The talk includes face and arm sensations, negative motor responses near premotor territories, and connections with motor, sensory, medial frontal, and insular regions. These observations explain why a stimulation effect involving the inferior frontal gyrus need not be aphasia. A naming trial may fail because the patient cannot execute speech, because attention is diverted by an induced sensation, or because a language operation is disturbed. That list is a teaching analysis of possible mechanisms, not a diagnosis from one failed trial. 

## Dorsolateral and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex

Francione's PAROS review of areas 8, 9, and 46 found relatively sparse directly elicited clinical phenomena and substantial limitations in anatomical and afterdischarge reporting. Posterior sampling nearer premotor regions yielded more overt responses than more anterior sampling. Eye deviation, interruption of movement, speech-related changes, and occasional subjective experiences appeared in the reviewed literature. Much of that literature used surface electrodes. [Stefano Francione — Prefrontal cortex : dorsolateral (BA8-9-46) & dorsomedial (BA8-9) cortices](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25371/prefrontal-cortex-dorsolateral-ba8-9-46-dorsomedial-ba8-9-cortices-stefano-francione)

The speaker also identified a task bias: finger tapping makes motor arrest visible, whereas routine observation may miss an effect on planning, working memory, or an internally maintained rule. Thus, “few overt responses” and “few relevant functions” are very different conclusions. A response distribution partly reflects what investigators asked patients to do.

Forced or intrusive thinking illustrates the other side of the problem. Popa and colleagues described three cases with intrusive thoughts during stimulation involving dorsolateral prefrontal cortex or nearby white matter. This establishes that the phenomenon has been observed, but the small series and gray/white-matter uncertainty prevent a precise thought-to-gyrus assignment. The related experiential literature is discussed in [Chapter 8](#chapter-8). [Popa et al., 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2016.00114/full). 

## Lateral premotor cortex, SMA proper, and pre-SMA

### Related territories with overlapping responses

Barba's teaching separates lateral premotor cortex from mesial supplementary motor territories. Pre-SMA is discussed in relation to higher-order initiation and planning, and SMA proper more closely in relation to execution. These functional descriptions are broad, and the boundary between SMA proper and the medial M1 leg representation can be difficult to assign from an anatomical label alone. [Carmen Barba — Premotor cortex: lateral (BA6) & mesial (BA6/ SMA-preSMA)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25372/premotor-cortex-lateral-ba6-mesial-ba6-sma-presma-carmen-barba)

The regional review includes tonic movements, somatosensory experiences, interrupted movement, and speech arrest or slowing. It also emphasizes overlap: the same broad response category can occur in more than one premotor territory. Older studies sometimes use “SMA” or “negative motor area” without enough detail to determine the modern subdivision. Apparent disagreement may therefore reflect different anatomy hidden under the same name. 

### Positive motor, negative motor, and urge

A positive motor response adds movement or contraction. A negative motor response interrupts ongoing voluntary movement. An urge to move is a reported experience that may occur without visible movement. These are not interchangeable, and a *negative motor response* is not a *negative stimulation result*.

Trevisi and colleagues studied 452 medial-frontal stimulations in 38 people and described positive motor, negative motor, somatosensory, and speech-related responses, with rare urges to move without overt movement. Their probabilistic analysis demonstrates organization at the cohort level while preserving overlap among response types. It is not a contact-by-contact prediction for a new patient. [Trevisi et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6259584/).

Chassagnon and colleagues also distinguished SMA proper, pre-SMA, and neighboring cingulate motor territories in an SEEG cohort. The reported stimulation responses did not predict postoperative neurological outcome in that study. Functional participation demonstrated by stimulation and outcome after a particular operation remain different questions. [Chassagnon et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18826345/). 

## Primary motor cortex

M1 occupies the precentral motor territory, including cortex within the central sulcus and the medial continuation. Kimiskidis's PAROS review emphasizes elementary motor responses, including tonic and clonic forms, while also noting reported sensory phenomena and inconsistent descriptions of whether induced seizures resembled habitual events. Surface and depth stimulation sample this region differently. [Vasilis Kimiskidis — M1 (BA4)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25374/m1-ba4-vasilis-kimiskidis)

The familiar motor homunculus is an orientation device. It should not obscure overlap, sulcal anatomy, the difference between a muscle contraction and a coordinated action, or the effect of stimulation conditions on the visible response. A sustained contraction, rhythmic movement, and suppression of ongoing muscle activity need distinct descriptions.

Physiology can reveal what inspection misses. Ikeda and colleagues examined motor-evoked responses and stimulus-linked silent periods with electromyography. Fotedar and colleagues used polygraphic recordings in a small series to characterize motor responses and, in a subset, the relation between EEG and EMG during induced seizures. These studies teach the value of retaining muscle and electrographic information rather than reducing every event to “motor positive.” [Ikeda et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10908200/); [Fotedar et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36966591/). 

## A teaching comparison: three superficially similar failures

Consider three hypothetical observations during an ongoing hand task:

- The hand stops moving, while the patient remains engaged and reports being unable to continue.
- The hand assumes a sustained posture that interferes with the intended action.
- The patient stops because an unfamiliar bodily sensation captures attention.

All three might look like a failed movement trial in a terse record. The first raises a negative-motor interpretation, the second describes an added motor phenomenon, and the third suggests an interruption secondary to an experience. The examples show why behavior, patient report, timing, and EEG context should remain available to the reader. They do not supply formal criteria for classifying a site.

The same principle applies to speech: failure to speak during stimulation does not automatically mean a lexical-language deficit. Frontal interpretation becomes more precise when the account identifies the interrupted operation rather than naming the lobe as its explanation.

Conference reviews contain heterogeneous studies and do not provide a uniform denominator for response frequency.

The linked primary studies retain their own methods and populations. # Chapter 15 — Cingulate, Parietal, and Occipital Regions

The regions in this chapter connect action, bodily experience, spatial orientation, and vision. Their stimulation findings are easier to understand when the anatomical subdivision and the kind of response are both named. “Parietal” can describe a focal hand sensation, an altered sense of body position, a calculation error, or a visual disturbance; it is too broad to explain any one of them.

A second theme is the value of null findings. Studies that report no obvious effect in a carefully bounded region help test claims made from more loosely labelled positive cases. They are particularly informative when the supposedly silent cortex is demonstrably active during a cognitive task. 

## Anterior, midcingulate, and subgenual regions

### Why the terminology changes the interpretation

Chassoux's PAROS presentation emphasizes the distinction between anterior cingulate cortex, midcingulate cortex, and their subdivisions. Older usage of “anterior cingulate” can include tissue now assigned to midcingulate territories. Subgenual and pregenual labels locate cortex relative to the genu of the corpus callosum; the terms should not be treated as synonyms for the entire medial frontal lobe. [Francine Chassoux — Subgenual, anterior & mid cingulate cortex (BA25-24-32)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25373/subgenual-anterior-mid-cingulate-cortex-ba25-24-32-francine-chassoux)

In the conference synthesis, affective and visceral/autonomic responses were concentrated toward anterior and pregenual territories, whereas complex motor behaviors were prominent in anterior midcingulate cortex. Positive and negative emotional experiences both occurred in the broader literature. The pattern suggests a gradient of contribution, not an emotion-only anterior region and a movement-only middle region.

Caruana and colleagues' retrospective intracerebral-stimulation study of 329 people provides a major primary source for that organization. Emotional, interoceptive, and autonomic responses were prominent in pregenual sectors, while complex motor behaviors were prominent in anterior midcingulate sectors. More posterior sectors included sensory and visual effects. These are distributions within the study's parcellation and sampling. [Caruana et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30107501/). 

### Affective experience and motor expression

Smiling, laughter, a pleasant feeling, abdominal discomfort, and fear can be related without being the same endpoint. Laughter with mirth includes both expression and felt emotion; laughter without reported mirth exposes a dissociation. The same distinction applies to a visible behavioral response accompanied by autonomic change: it does not identify the patient's subjective experience unless that experience was elicited and described.

Chassoux discusses these combinations in the cingulate literature. Read with [Chapter 9](#chapter-9), they support an account that preserves both experience and expression. Read with [Chapter 10](#chapter-10), they also show why an integrated action can involve medial motor networks without being reducible to a single muscle representation. 

## Primary somatosensory cortex

S1 lies posterior to the central sulcus and comprises areas 3, 1, and 2, with further subdivisions of area 3. Nilsson's PAROS teaching contrasts the often relatively circumscribed body distribution of S1 responses with some of the broader bodily sensations reported from insular stimulation. That is a useful clinical tendency, not a rule that makes every focal paresthesia an S1 response. [Josefin Nilsson — S1 (BA1-2-3)](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25375/s1-ba1-2-3-josefin-nilsson)

Somatotopy organizes the question: which part of the body is affected, on which side, and with what quality? The response should still be described before being assigned to a body-map label. Tingling, perceived movement, altered position, and an observed movement are different observations even when they concern the same hand.

Sun and colleagues examined 244 S1 stimulation sites in 33 people and reported responses from 198, predominantly in sulcal cortex. SEEG can therefore extend the sampling beyond what is visible on the exposed postcentral surface. A diagram of the lateral homunculus alone is an incomplete description of the tissue assessed. [Sun et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33770611/).

The conference review also notes motor as well as sensory responses in studies assigned to S1. This makes the electrode pair's relation to the central sulcus and adjacent motor cortex important. It does not justify relabelling a motor effect as sensory merely because the contact was catalogued under S1. The project's related same-center S1 reports may overlap in participants and are not counted as independent replications. 

## Superior parietal lobule, precuneus, and intraparietal sulcus

### Anatomical orientation

Nevalainen and Vanhanen distinguish the superior parietal lobule on the lateral surface from the precuneus on the medial surface. Areas 5 and 7 are common orientation labels; the assignment of bordering area 31 varies across accounts. The intraparietal sulcus separates superior and inferior parietal territories and has its own heterogeneous functional organization. These regions should not be collapsed into a single “posterior parietal” point. [Päivi Nevalainen & Jukka Vanhanen — Superior parietal lobule (except S1) & precuneus](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25376/superior-parietal-lobule-except-s1-precuneus-paivi-nevalainen-jukka-vanhanen)

The talk relates them to visuomotor integration, reaching, haptic perception, spatial attention, and the relationship between visual space and the body. These background functions help explain why stimulation reports can include disturbed body position, vestibular experiences, visual changes, or disruption of goal-directed actions. A whole-parietal SEEG study of 172 people likewise reported multimodal effects; its broad distribution does not establish the same response profile in every parietal subdivision. [Balestrini et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26129769/). 

### Precuneus is not synonymous with default mode network

“Posteromedial cortex” and “default mode network” are often used loosely in discussions of self-related experience. Anatomical and functional boundaries matter. Foster and Parvizi found no observable behavioral or subjectively reported effects within their strictly defined posteromedial region during standard functional mapping in 25 people, even at sites that showed electrophysiological activity during autobiographical recall. Apparent effects at boundary-crossing pairs required separate treatment. [Foster and Parvizi, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5317378/).

Lyu and colleagues later reported bodily dissociation with stimulation of anterior precuneus sites in nine people. The implicated region was outside the conventional default mode network, although connected with it. These studies therefore should not be reduced to a simple contradiction between “precuneus causes dissociation” and “precuneus is silent.” They differ in the precise region and experimental question. [Lyu et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37295420/).

The teaching point is that a network name is not a substitute for showing where the pair lies. A region can also participate in a task without routine stimulation there producing a spontaneous report. [Chapter 8](#chapter-8) discusses the experiential distinctions. 

## Inferior parietal lobule: supramarginal and angular gyri

Van der Salm's PAROS review treats the supramarginal and angular gyri separately. The teaching includes language, praxis, numerical processing, finger identification, left–right discrimination, body representation, and sensory experiences. Evidence was uneven: dominant-hemisphere task studies were more informative than the sparse literature on some nondominant functions. [Sandra van der Salm — Inferior parietal lobule & angularis gyrus](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25377/inferior-parietal-lobule-angularis-gyrus-sandra-van-der-salm)

The reported disturbances depend on the task. A calculation error is not the same as inability to read the number; an impaired gesture is not the same as weakness; inability to name a finger is not automatically failure to recognize it. The conference review itself notes that some reports labelled “apraxia” without describing exactly what failed. Detailed behavior is necessary to understand those cases.

Two well-known experiments illustrate different contributions. Desmurget and colleagues reported movement intention during inferior-parietal stimulation in seven awake-surgery participants. Blanke and colleagues repeatedly elicited altered body and vestibular experiences in one patient during right-angular-gyrus stimulation. The former addresses intention and action; the latter addresses bodily self-location and perception. Neither establishes a comprehensive angular-gyrus syndrome or a universal hemispheric rule. [Desmurget et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19423830/); [Blanke et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12239558/). 

## Posterior cingulate cortex

Imbach's PAROS synthesis describes the posterior cingulate as differently connected from anterior and midcingulate cortex. Across the reviewed literature, vestibular, visual, bodily, and less specific experiential phenomena appeared among the posterior findings, while language effects were uncommon. The talk emphasizes that Brodmann and anatomical definitions need reconciliation before studies can be pooled. [Lukas Imbach — Posterior cingulate cortex](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25378/posterior-cingulate-cortex-lukas-imbach)

A posterior-cingulate SEEG cohort reported both direct-stimulation responses and spontaneous seizure characteristics with propagation. These are complementary observations: a response elicited from a sampled pair and a symptom appearing after seizure spread answer different questions. The symptom can reflect recruitment of a connected region rather than uniquely identify where the spontaneous seizure began. [Yan et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38772097/).

The null posteromedial findings described above remain relevant. A broad label covering posterior cingulate, retrosplenial, and neighboring medial parietal territories may combine regions that differ in both connectivity and elicitable responses. The literature is best read with those anatomical boundaries visible. 

## Mesial and lateral occipital cortex

### Start with the visual experience

A phosphene is a perceived light without the corresponding external light source. More elaborate phenomena can include geometric forms, recognizable objects or scenes, or distortion of something actually present. A patient who reports that an object changes size is describing a different event from a patient who sees a new object. Recognition failure is different again: the visual input may be present but its identity cannot be accessed during the tested condition. These descriptive distinctions are developed in [Chapter 7](#chapter-7).

Martineau's mesial-occipital teaching uses the calcarine sulcus, cuneus, and lingual gyrus as the principal landmarks. The cuneus lies above the calcarine sulcus and the lingual gyrus below it. The talk reviews the general retinotopic principle that posterior calcarine cortex represents more central vision and anterior portions more peripheral vision. That background anatomy helps frame a reported percept; it does not assign a visual-field coordinate to a poorly localized electrode pair. [Laurence Martineau — Mesial occipital cortex](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25379/mesial-occipital-cortex-laurence-martineau)

### Gradients and exceptions

The mesial-occipital conference review describes elementary and relatively static phenomena in calcarine territories, with color and motion features distributed differently across lingual and cuneal samples. The lateral-occipital review by Chipaux-Raffo also found elementary hallucinations prominent, with rarer intermediate or complex phenomena and occasional face-recognition disturbance. The small and uneven samples, and incomplete subregional localization, limit strong comparisons between these distributions. [Mathilde Chipaux-Raffo — Lateral occipital cortex](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25380/lateral-occipital-cortex-mathilde-chipaux-raffo)

Jonas and colleagues separated simple, intermediate, and complex visual phenomena in a depth-stimulation study and reported a study-level hemispheric asymmetry. Lee and colleagues described form, color, and motion effects with surface stimulation. Together, these studies support a richer account than “occipital equals flashing lights,” while retaining major effects of sampled region and method. [Jonas et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6869193/); [Lee et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10690975/). 

### Why absence of a negative visual response is difficult to interpret

Martineau explicitly noted a lack of negative visual signs in the reviewed DCS set despite such signs being represented in the seizure-semiology literature. The difference could reflect the question, task, sampling, or reporting rather than an inability of stimulation to affect visual function. A spontaneously reported light is easy to notice; a subtle field or recognition deficit requires an appropriate assessment.

A visual experience, a formal field deficit, ictal visual symptoms, and postoperative visual outcome therefore remain distinct. The regional studies in this chapter do not convert one into an individual prediction of another. Restoring this distinction adds useful detail: it explains *why* a quiet trial cannot replace the visual assessment relevant to the clinical question.

# Chapter 16 — Integrating Findings: What Happened, What It Means, and What Remains Open

A useful stimulation account lets another clinician reconstruct the reasoning. It describes the event, gives enough context to understand how it was obtained, and explains the inference without presenting that inference as another directly observed fact. This chapter develops that distinction through teaching examples.

The framework is an editorial synthesis of the preceding chapters. It is not a mandatory reporting template or a confidence score. Its purpose is to make complex evidence discussable: a colleague should be able to agree with the observation while questioning the interpretation. 

## Begin with the question the stimulation addressed

Functional mapping, seizure induction, and network probing can all occur within an invasive evaluation, but success means something different in each setting. In a language task, the finding may be a reproducible change in a tested operation. In seizure induction, the finding may be an electroclinical event compared with the person's spontaneous seizures. With single pulses, the response may be a recorded potential or spectral change without any reported experience.

The international practice survey by Cockle and colleagues describes considerable variation in mapping practice and separates functional responses, afterdischarges, and epileptogenicity assessment. This is a reason to state the purpose explicitly rather than using an unqualified “positive.” [Cockle et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10472359/).

For example, “positive at the temporal pair” could mean that the patient heard a sound, made a naming error, developed an afterdischarge, or had a habitual seizure. Each is potentially informative, but they are not equivalent evidence. The original purpose also determines the importance of a missing observation: absence of a spoken complaint cannot establish that language was intact if no language operation was tested. 

## Separate the finding from the explanation

Three layers are useful when reading a summary:

| Layer | What belongs here | Illustrative distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Finding | Patient report, observed behavior, task performance, recorded physiology | “Reported a rising abdominal sensation” is the experience described |
| Context | Anatomy, task, baseline, stimulation conditions, timing, electrographic evolution | The experience occurred during a train; the visible EEG onset may have been elsewhere |
| Interpretation | The proposed relationship to function, habitual semiology, or the seizure hypothesis | “Resembles the habitual initial aura” is a comparison, not proof of the onset site |

The table is a reading aid. Its value is that it prevents an anatomical interpretation from replacing the underlying event. A report can preserve a specific sensation even when its mechanism remains unsettled. Equally, it can record a focal task failure without claiming that the whole language system has been mapped. 

## Functional findings: understand the failed operation

Functional interpretation depends on what the patient could do before stimulation, what changed during it, and what competing explanations remain. A naming error may arise at a perceptual, semantic, lexical, phonological, articulatory, or attentional stage. The anatomical label helps formulate possibilities; the task behavior helps distinguish them. [Chapter 11](#chapter-11) explains this decomposition in more detail.

Repetition adds information about consistency, but consistency is not a universal definition of a positive site. The survey found variation in repeat-trial practice and criteria. Young and colleagues' language study used a specified definition of a stimulation hit and did not count ictal language disturbance in the same way. A summary should retain that kind of distinction rather than translating every repeated error into an identical category across centers. [Young et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8252889/).

A functional finding also concerns the tested conditions. “No naming change observed” is narrower and more informative than “no language.” The first tells the reader what was assessed; the second can imply that comprehension, reading, repetition, discourse, and other operations were all evaluated. This is a principle of accurate description, not a substitute for the institution's formal mapping criteria. 

## Induced events: compare several dimensions

### Clinical resemblance

An induced experience can reproduce an aura, a fragment of the habitual sequence, or a fuller event. It may also include unfamiliar features. A within-patient study by Mahizhnan and colleagues explicitly distinguished habitual and atypical events and aura, fragment, and full-seizure expressions. These categories make the comparison more precise than a single yes/no judgment of concordance. [Mahizhnan et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41603150/).

The patient's recognition is meaningful, particularly for subjective symptoms that an observer cannot see. It remains one component of the comparison. A familiar abdominal sensation alone does not establish that the complete induced event reproduced the usual electroclinical sequence.

### Electrographic resemblance

The pair delivering stimulation, the contacts showing the earliest recorded induced change, and the contacts showing spontaneous seizure onset are separate anatomical observations. Reindl and colleagues analyzed these separately in a selected surgical cohort. Their work illustrates why the phrase “the seizure was induced from this pair” should not silently become “the seizure began in the tissue between this pair.” [Reindl et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-92241-z).

Artifact and limited sampling can leave the earliest phase unresolved. A careful explanation can acknowledge that uncertainty while still describing the later evolution and clinical resemblance. Stating what cannot be seen is more useful than assigning a precise onset solely to make the account feel complete. 

## A worked teaching example

The following is hypothetical and contains no patient material.

A person usually describes an abdominal sensation followed by a brief change in familiarity and impaired responsiveness. During stimulation of a sampled insular pair, an abdominal sensation is reported and recognized as familiar. The familiarity change and impaired responsiveness do not occur. The available recording does not demonstrate an evolving electroclinical seizure.

A defensible description is that stimulation elicited a recognized component of the habitual subjective sequence. It supports a relationship between the perturbed network and that symptom. It does not, by itself, reproduce the whole habitual seizure, demonstrate its spontaneous onset, or decide the full epileptogenic-zone hypothesis.

Now change one feature: an evolving event occurs, with a similar sequence but an earliest recorded change in a sampled mesial temporal region. The clinical resemblance is now broader, while the anatomical comparison becomes more complex. The stimulated pair, recorded induced onset, and spontaneous onset need to remain separately visible. The change in evidence should change the discussion; it should not be hidden under the same word “concordant.”

This example applies the distinctions in Chapters 3, 7, and 13 and the induced-seizure cohort literature. It is not a method for selecting a treatment target. 

## When findings agree—and when they do not

Agreement across anatomy, a well-characterized response, habitual semiology, and spontaneous recordings can make an explanation more coherent. The strength of that coherence depends on whether the observations contribute independent information. Several labels derived from the same induced event are not several independent confirmations.

Disagreement can be informative. A familiar symptom with different recorded evolution may indicate that the symptom can be reached through more than one network route. A task deficit near a proposed treatment region may raise a function-related concern even when it says little about seizure generation. An absent reported effect may reflect the task or sampling rather than resolve a disputed anatomical hypothesis.

These are possible interpretations to examine, not automatic conclusions. The useful summary states the specific agreement or discrepancy and the remaining alternatives. Numerical weights would require validation that this manual's source package does not provide. 

## Keep the endpoint visible

A cohort association with seizure outcome is different from a demonstration of functional participation. Likewise, a relation between language-positive stimulation and language decline after RF-TC concerns a particular functional endpoint and treatment context. It does not establish seizure benefit. [Chapter 17](#chapter-17) examines these study designs directly.

The final interpretive paragraph should therefore answer the original question at the level the evidence supports. It can explain what was learned, which observations support that conclusion, and which uncertainties remain. It need not force every response into a confident localizer, and it should not conceal an informative finding merely because a stronger conclusion is unavailable.

The sources are the international practice survey; Young's language-task study; Reindl's induced-seizure cohort; Mahizhnan's within-patient semiology comparison; and the source-backed distinctions developed in Chapters 1–15. Study definitions remain study-specific.

The final 2025 ACNS technical standard is identified in Chapter 2. Detailed reporting passages used in an earlier project audit came from its 2024 draft; this chapter does not attribute a mandatory report structure to the final standard. Source verification and named clinical signoff remain separate.

# Chapter 17 — Stimulation Findings in Surgical Planning: Studies, Methods, and Limits

Stimulation findings enter surgical discussions in several forms: a habitual electroclinical seizure induced by a train, an evoked response following a single pulse, disruption of a language task, or a research association with pathology. These observations can inform the same discussion while measuring different things.

The central question is therefore not whether they can be discussed together, but what each adds. The studies below examine seizure outcome, network responses, language decline after radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RF-TC), and histopathology. The checked source packet does not validate a combined patient-level decision tool. That limit leaves substantial room to learn from the individual studies. 

## Repetitive stimulation and induced seizures

### What the major cohorts actually compare

Cuello Oderiz and colleagues studied 103 patients at two centers. Their analysis concerned typical electroclinical seizures induced during SEEG and the relation between stimulation-informed seizure-onset regions, resection, and surgical outcome. Clinical resemblance accompanied by electrographic evolution was central to the study's definition; an isolated aura or afterdischarge was not automatically the same endpoint. Greater resection overlap with the stimulation-informed onset region was associated with favorable outcome. [Cuello Oderiz et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6563597/).

Reindl and colleagues examined a smaller retrospective cohort of 20 surgical patients selected for having at least one stimulation-induced seizure. They compared stimulated contacts, induced-seizure onset contacts, spontaneous seizure-onset contacts, semiology, and resection. Favorable associations were reported, but the selection means that the study cannot tell us how an otherwise similar population without an induced seizure would perform. [Reindl et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-92241-z).

These studies make a clinically relevant distinction: the *site that can trigger an event* and the *site where the event is first recorded* need not be identical. An outcome analysis also depends on how each site set was defined and how treatment overlap was measured. A statement about one set cannot simply be transferred to the other. 

### Semiology and outcome are separate endpoints

Mahizhnan and colleagues found substantial within-patient semiologic concordance between spontaneous and stimulation-induced events, using distinctions among habitual or atypical events and aura, fragment, or fuller seizure expressions. Dominant induced-seizure semiologic subtype was not associated with surgical outcome in that study. A detailed resemblance analysis can therefore be informative without supplying a prognostic classification. [Mahizhnan et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41603150/).

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Fox and colleagues pooled three studies for an outcome association and reported a risk ratio of 1.76 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.96–3.23. This interval is imprecise and includes the null value. The result neither establishes a dependable outcome benefit nor proves that induced-seizure information has no value. It describes the uncertainty in the pooled evidence. [Fox et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40466207/).

The cohorts and the meta-analysis should be read together: individual favorable associations motivate further study, while selection, definitions, treatment decisions, and limited precision constrain their transfer to a new patient. The manual does not assign a fixed weight to concordance. 

## Single-pulse electrical stimulation: what is being measured?

Single-pulse electrical stimulation (SPES) perturbs a sampled site and records responses elsewhere. Repeating separated pulses can make an evoked response easier to characterize. The response may be examined as voltage over time, a change in signal power across frequencies, or a network constructed from responses between sites. These are different views of the recording, not interchangeable biomarkers. [Ioannis Stavropoulos — Single pulse DCS to define the epileptogenic zone](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25387/single-pulse-dcs-to-define-the-epileptogenic-zone-ioannis-stavropoulos)

A **cortico-cortical evoked potential** describes a time-domain response following cortical stimulation. A **spectral response** describes a change in activity within specified frequency bands and time windows. A **network measure** summarizes relationships among multiple sampled sites. None of these terms, by itself, says that a site is epileptogenic. 

### Early, delayed, and repetitive responses

In the SPES literature reviewed at PAROS, early responses occur promptly after the pulse and are used to study effective connectivity. Delayed responses arise later and can have epileptiform morphology; some studies also describe repetitive complexes. Their meaning depends on the study's timing, morphology, and analysis definitions.

Valentín and colleagues' prospective 40-patient study associated abnormal SPES responses with structural pathology and found better seizure outcomes when regions showing those responses were completely resected. This supports investigation of the relationship between evoked physiology, tissue abnormality, and treatment overlap. It does not establish that every large or late response has the same significance. [Valentín et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16239178/).

Mouthaan and colleagues studied 12 patients and specifically asked whether early responses also contained useful information. Their study distinguished early responses before 100 ms from delayed responses after 100 ms and examined time-domain and time-frequency properties. Early responses, including high-frequency content, showed associations with seizure-onset and propagation regions. The 100-ms division is that study's descriptive convention, not a universal clinical cutoff adopted here. The work also challenges a simplistic equation of “early” with entirely physiological and “late” with entirely pathological. [Mouthaan et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26377063/). 

### Spectral responses and centrality

Hays and colleagues studied 27 patients using cortico-cortical spectral responses and graph measures. In a graph, sampled sites are represented as nodes and response relationships as connections. Centrality is a family of measures describing how prominently a node is connected or positioned within that constructed network; it is not a direct measurement of the importance of a piece of tissue to seizure freedom.

Their analysis associated greater spectral-response centrality with seizure-onset sites and examined the relationship to treatment and outcome. The models were developed using successful-outcome cases, and the cohort included different treatments. Sampling, the chosen response definition, model construction, and outcome classification all influence the result. The reported performance is research evidence for the method, not a ready-made contact-ranking rule. [Hays et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11401764/). 

## Why a connection is not automatically a target

SPES can show that perturbing one sampled site produces a response at another. This helps investigate network organization. It does not alone determine whether the connection initiates seizures, carries their spread, supports normal function, or does several of these things under different conditions.

As a hypothetical example, a strongly connected temporal site could appear prominent in an evoked-response network while also participating in language or memory. The network measure and the functional observation would address different properties of the tissue. Neither disappears because the other is present. Turning the pair of observations into a treatment decision requires patient-specific clinical judgment and evidence beyond a network label.

This is why the methods table one might build from these studies should compare constructs and endpoints, rather than rank “better” and “worse” contacts. The relevant question is what the measure was tested against, in whom, and with what outcome. 

## Functional findings and RF-TC: the language study

RF-TC produces lesions through implanted electrodes. Functional stimulation before treatment asks about participation in a tested function, whereas postoperative assessment asks whether performance changed after the actual intervention. The perturbation and the lesion differ in duration, spatial effects, and clinical context.

Cockle and colleagues prospectively studied 36 patients at two centers and assessed language at approximately three months after SEEG RF-TC. Eight patients showed decline on a language measure. RF-TC involving sites identified as language-positive by DCS was associated with a greater likelihood of language decline. The result was specific to the studied language tasks, site definitions, treatment decisions, and assessment. [Cockle et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12404774/).

The study makes functional stimulation relevant to a concrete postoperative endpoint, but it does not provide an individual risk percentage or prove safety from a negative language response. Nor can a language result be generalized automatically to memory, vision, motor function, or seizure benefit. Those require their own evidence. Liakina's PAROS review similarly highlights inconsistent reporting of stimulation findings and treatment-site details across RF-TC reports. [Tatjana Liakina — The correlation between results of SEEG cortical stimulation and planning of RF-TC procedures](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25388/the-correlation-between-results-of-seeg-cortical-stimulation-and-planning-of-rf-tc-procedures-tatjana-liakina) 

## Histology: association, signature, and proposal

These three ideas need separation. An **association** means that a stimulation feature occurs differently across pathological groups. A **signature** would require sufficiently reliable and specific performance to identify the pathology from the feature in a new case. A **proposal** describes an investigation that has not yet supplied the completed evidence for that claim.

Zhao and colleagues' 80-patient study reported an association between focal cortical dysplasia type II and seizure inducibility. That is completed pathology–inducibility evidence, but it does not establish a histology-specific induced-seizure pattern. Valentín's structural-pathology findings concern a different paradigm—single-pulse responses—and should not be merged with repetitive-DCS seizure morphology. [Zhao et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37480785/); [Valentín et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16239178/).

Tsalouchidou's PAROS communication presents a multicenter proposal examining induced seizure-onset patterns, stimulation context, reproducibility, resemblance to spontaneous seizures, histology, and outcome. It identifies an important unresolved question. Its proposed design is not a completed validation study. [Pania Tsalouchidou — Direct cortical stimulation-induced seizures across histological subtypes: a multicenter study of seizure-onset patterns, histology, and surgical Outcome](https://projector-web.gr/myrtaly/en/paros-epilepsy-2026/videos-on-demand/25386/direct-cortical-stimulation-induced-seizures-across-histological-subtypes-a-multicenter-study-of-seizure-onset-patterns-histology-and-surgical-outcome-pania-tsalouchidou)

Studies linking *spontaneous* SEEG onset patterns with pathology provide relevant background, but transfer to *stimulation-induced* morphology needs direct testing. The stimulus itself and the recruited network may alter the observed pattern. The sources checked here do not establish a histology-specific repetitive-DCS signature or a patient-level pathology rule. 

## Reading these studies in a multidisciplinary discussion

The evidence becomes more useful when its purpose is explicit. An induced seizure may contribute to an electroclinical hypothesis; a single-pulse measure may describe network physiology; a language-positive finding may raise a function-related concern; a histology association may motivate research interpretation. Each can be discussed without pretending they are measurements on a common scale.

A concise synthesis can therefore state the observation, the comparison that supports its relevance, and the uncertainty that prevents a stronger inference. The unresolved step is the validated translation from these heterogeneous observations to individual treatment benefit and risk. The manual teaches the evidence leading up to that step and leaves patient-specific treatment decisions with the clinical team.
